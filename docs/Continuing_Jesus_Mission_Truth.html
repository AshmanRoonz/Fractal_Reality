Jesus came to bear witness to the truth. He exposed the lies that make evil possible. He called others to continue his witness. What would it mean to take that mission seriously today?

An essay by Ashman Roonz · fractalreality.ca

“To this end was I born, and for this cause came I into the world, that I should bear witness unto the truth.”

Jesus · John 18:37 (KJV)

The proposition

What if we have been overlooking the enemy's method?

Christians have long understood Jesus as the one who confronts sin, defeats evil, and reconciles humanity to God. But the Gospels also make a striking claim about how he carries out that mission: he bears witness to the truth.

In the Gospel of John, Jesus names the Devil as a liar. He says truth makes people free. He confronts hypocrisy and the concealment of wrongdoing. At his trial, the narrative depicts authorities seeking false testimony against him. And before Pilate, Jesus declares that bearing witness to the truth is a purpose for which he came into the world.

These are not isolated details. They form a consistent biblical pattern. Deception draws people into wrongdoing, supplies its excuses, and protects it from exposure. Truth removes those protections and opens the possibility of repentance, freedom, and restoration.

Our central claim

Making truth-telling safer than lying is a modern way of carrying forward a central dimension of Jesus's mission: bearing witness to the truth, confronting deception, and making restoration possible.

This is not a claim that Jesus taught our modern slogan word for word, nor that truth-telling exhausts the Christian gospel. It is a claim that a Christian society should make its loyalty to truth visible in the way it treats people.

I · The biblical pattern

From the first deception to the witness of Christ

1. Genesis: the serpent begins with a lie

In Genesis 3, humanity's first act of disobedience is preceded by a distortion of God's command and intentions. The serpent does not begin by using force. He begins by persuading human beings to mistrust what they have been told and to imagine that disobedience will bring a better outcome.

“The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat.”

Genesis 3:13 (KJV)

The narrative suggests a pattern through which moral failure can develop:

01Deception
02False understanding
03Wrongdoing
04Alienation

Deception is not the whole explanation of sin: the account also involves desire and choice. But it is crucial to the temptation's form. Before the wrong action, there is a wrong story about why the action is acceptable.

2. Jesus identifies the Devil with lying

“When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it.”

John 8:44 (KJV)

Jesus also calls the Devil a murderer in the same verse. Lying and destruction appear in one moral portrait. In Revelation 12:9, Satan is described as deceiving the whole world.

Evil here is not only the visible harm done to others. It is also the falsehood that can lead people toward that harm and disguise it as necessity, justice, loyalty, or goodness.

3. Jesus openly declares his mission to truth

Standing before Pontius Pilate, Jesus makes the declaration that should frame this entire discussion:

“To this end was I born, and for this cause came I into the world, that I should bear witness unto the truth.”

John 18:37 (KJV)

This is not our attempt to invent a mission for Jesus. It is one of the Gospel's clearest statements about how Jesus understands his coming into the world. To bear witness means to testify—to stand for what is true, even when it costs something.

In John's theology, truth is more than accurate information: it is bound to God's revelation in Christ, God's faithfulness, and the life offered through him. The call to truth is therefore not merely an instruction to correct other people's factual errors. It is a call to align one's life with God's reality.

Jesus continues:

“And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.”

John 8:32 (KJV)

The context connects this freedom to release from slavery to sin. Truth is not presented merely as information to possess. It is liberating.

4. Jesus exposes hypocrisy: the lie that goodness can hide evil

In Matthew 23, Jesus condemns a form of religious hypocrisy in which outward righteousness conceals inward corruption. The problem is not religion itself; it is the use of religious appearances to escape genuine moral accountability.

In John 3:19–21, evildoers are portrayed as avoiding the light lest their deeds be exposed. This makes a powerful connection: evil often protects itself by making exposure dangerous.

If a community punishes the person who speaks truth more readily than the person who commits wrongdoing, it has reproduced the very problem Jesus confronted.

5. The trial of Jesus shows deception serving power

“Now the chief priests, and elders, and all the council, sought false witness against Jesus, to put him to death.”

Matthew 26:59 (KJV)

According to Matthew's account, some of the authorities involved in the proceedings sought false testimony. The legal and religious process, meant to serve justice, was turned toward a preferred outcome.

And in John 18:38, after Jesus speaks of truth, Pilate responds with the question: “What is truth?” The scene puts the witness to truth face to face with the authority that must decide what to do with him.

We should not flatten the historical complexity of the crucifixion or assign collective blame to Jewish people. The Gospels place Jesus's execution in a specific setting of Roman power, conflict, political pressure, and the actions of particular people. The moral warning is universal: religious and political authorities alike can betray justice when protecting power matters more than truth.

The mission in our time

Make truth-telling
safer than lying.

Not a replacement for the gospel. A public and practical expression of what it means to bear witness to truth in a world where deception can be rewarded.

II · The continuation

Jesus did not call us to admire the truth from a distance

Jesus's witness was not presented as an isolated performance to be remembered while ordinary human life continued unchanged. His followers were also commissioned to testify.

“And ye also shall bear witness, because ye have been with me from the beginning.”

John 15:27 (KJV)

“As my Father hath sent me, even so send I you.”

John 20:21 (KJV)

These passages do not mean that Jesus's saving work was unfinished or that later Christians replace him. They mean that the witness inaugurated by Jesus is carried into the world through the people he sends.

Christian discipleship is therefore not simply agreeing that Jesus told the truth. It is living in such a way that truth, love, confession, and restoration become possible for other people.

To continue his mission is to refuse the idea that a good cause permits us to deceive, conceal wrongdoing, or silence those who expose it.

Truth is not the same as claiming certainty

None of us possesses God's complete perspective. We can be sincere and mistaken. We can misunderstand a passage, misjudge a person, or misremember an event. A commitment to truth therefore requires humility: willingness to listen, investigate, repent, and correct what we get wrong.

1 John 1:8–9 explicitly links self-deception with denying our sin, and confession with forgiveness and cleansing. A Christian who cannot admit error is not demonstrating superior devotion to truth.

The distinction that changes everything

We do not continue Jesus's witness by claiming we are never wrong. We continue it by refusing to make our own wrongness untouchable.

Truth must be joined to love

Paul's instruction in Ephesians 4:15 and 4:25 brings both duties together: speak truth in love and put away lying, because we belong to one another.

That last reason matters. We are not isolated contestants trying to defeat one another in argument. We are people whose lives affect each other. When deception damages trust, it harms the relationships through which a community lives.

Truth without compassion can be wielded as a weapon. Compassion without accountability can be used as an excuse for concealment. A mature Christian community must not choose one at the expense of the other.

III · The deeper theory

Why deception keeps evil alive

The Bible does not literally define every kind of evil as a lie. In Mark 7:20–23, Jesus names deceit alongside murder, theft, pride, and other evils. The claim made here is more precise: deception is a recurring mechanism through which wrongdoing gains permission and survives correction.

A person harms another and tells themselves the harm was deserved. An institution conceals abuse and says it must protect its reputation. A leader suppresses testimony and says the community's unity depends on silence.

The same pattern appears whenever self-preservation is promoted above truth. A false justification excuses the harm; a further deception shields it from scrutiny; and those who ask questions become the threat.

Deception permits

It turns “I want this” into “I am justified in doing this,” even when another person will suffer.

Deception conceals

It hides evidence, distorts testimony, and protects the person or structure benefiting from wrongdoing.

Deception multiplies

One lie may demand others, until an entire relationship or institution becomes organized around avoiding exposure.

Truth permits repair

It allows the wrong to be named, responsibility to be taken, and justice and reconciliation to begin.

Christian thinkers have approached evil from other angles too, including Augustine's understanding of evil as a privation or corruption of good. This essay does not displace those accounts. It proposes a complementary account of how evil operates in relationships and institutions.

IV · A practical Christian response

What would it take to continue this mission?

It is easy to condemn lying as an individual act. It is harder—and more necessary—to build conditions under which honest speech, confession, correction, and justice can survive.

  1. Make confession possible without humiliation.

    Hold people accountable, but do not make the admission of error automatically more punishing than concealment. Repentance must be possible without pretending harm did not happen.

  2. Protect people who truthfully report wrongdoing.

    Investigate fairly, protect against retaliation, and do not confuse criticism of leaders with betrayal of the community.

  3. Distinguish error from deliberate deception.

    Being wrong is a human limitation; knowingly manufacturing falsehood or suppressing evidence is a different moral choice. Correction requires room for uncertainty.

  4. Hold authority accountable to the same truth.

    No pastor, institution, politician, parent, or movement should be exempt from evidence, just process, and the duty to correct mistakes.

  5. Tell the truth in love, not as domination.

    Be accurate, humble, proportionate, and compassionate. Protect privacy and the vulnerable; honesty does not require handing sensitive information to those who intend harm.

  6. Choose correction over image management.

    When facts contradict our story, revise the story. Let truth matter more than winning, belonging, status, or saving face.

These principles cannot guarantee a world without lies. They can make deception harder to sustain and honest restoration more possible.

V · A declaration

We will continue the witness to truth.

Jesus came to bear witness to the truth.

He challenged hypocrisy. He confronted falsehood. He called people out of darkness and into light. He taught that truth makes us free. And he sent his followers to bear witness.

We will not claim to possess every truth. We will not confuse our certainty with God's authority. We will remain willing to be corrected.

We will not call deception noble because it serves our interests. We will not protect a reputation at the expense of a harmed person. We will not make confession more dangerous than concealment or punish honest questions as disloyalty.

We will seek truth with humility, speak truth with love, confront wrongdoing with courage, and make room for repentance and repair.

We cannot take Jesus's place. But we can answer his call.

For readers and scholars

Textual foundation & limits of the thesis

What the argument claims. John 18:37 explicitly presents Jesus's purpose as testimony to truth; John 15:27 and 20:21 present his disciples as continuing his witness and mission. This essay argues that creating conditions favorable to truth-telling is a coherent contemporary application of those texts.

What it does not claim. Jesus's whole mission cannot be reduced to modern norms of transparency, and the Bible does not identify every evil act with a literal lie. The Gospels also speak centrally of the kingdom of God, love, forgiveness, salvation, the cross, and resurrection. “Make truth-telling safer than lying” is our ethical formulation, not a quotation attributed to Jesus.

A difficult question. Biblical narratives involving the Hebrew midwives (Exodus 1) and Rahab (Joshua 2) complicate any simple rule about every misleading statement. Christian moral traditions disagree on how these passages bear on lying, protection of life, and duties owed to aggressors. This essay focuses chiefly on deception used to justify or conceal wrongdoing.

  • Augustine, On Lying and Enchiridion; Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II–II, q. 110 — classical Christian discussions of lying, truth, and evil.
