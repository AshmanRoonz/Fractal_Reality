## Prologue: The Part That Felt Like the Whole

Sometimes a stray thought is enough.

I think about my trauma, and a familiar surge begins in the center of my chest. I experience it as adrenaline spreading outward. It fills my body and takes over my attention. In that moment, the reaction can feel like the whole of me.

I know the difference between a memory and what is happening now. Yet knowing that difference does not always stop the feeling. The reaction can begin before I have chosen to spend any time with the thought.

I have some influence over it. Sometimes it feels as though I can turn down a valve. Doing that is challenging. What helps me is a particular thought, joined to a particular shift in attention:

My trauma is part of me. I am the whole.

As I remember this, I try to feel my whole body. I include more than the center of my chest. There is more of me here to notice than the place where the surge first attracts my attention.

The thought becomes something I can experience. My attention has somewhere else to go without requiring me to deny what hurts. I can acknowledge the reaction while noticing the larger life in which it is occurring.

That is what I mean when I say the trauma is part of me. I am not assigning it to one small location or pretending its effects are small. A part can affect an entire whole. The distinction concerns what the reaction is allowed to stand for. It can be intense, widespread, and important while remaining one experience within my life.

My life also includes curiosity, affection, creativity, values, and possibilities. Those aspects of me do not cease to exist when I have trouble feeling them.

I am describing something that helps me. Other people may experience their bodies and histories differently. The value of this experience for the book is the question it brings into view: how do we understand a whole when one of its parts occupies nearly all our attention?

That question reaches beyond trauma. It reaches into identity, relationships, work, and our place in the world. A role can become the main way we describe ourselves. A conflict can dominate our understanding of a relationship. An institution can judge a person almost entirely by the function it expects them to perform.

The relation between a person and a role deserves examination. So does the relation between a person and a feeling. What belongs to a life, and what makes that life one?

The same question also turns outward. I am a whole person, but my life takes place within a world that sustains and affects me. I belong to Earth. I participate in relationships. I exist within Reality as a whole.

This is the starting point of the book: being whole and being part can be true together.

The words are simple. Their consequences require care. We need to understand what counts as a whole, what kind of connection makes something a part, and how much can legitimately follow from either description.

I call my way of approaching these questions the Circumpunct Framework. Its symbol is a point inside a circle: ⊙. It gives me a way to think about a center, a boundary, and the relationships through which a life holds together and participates in its surroundings.

The symbol is a beginning. The work is to explain what it means and where it helps us understand something real.

# Part I: Recognizing a Whole

## Chapter 1: One Life, Many Parts

You answer to one name. You speak of your life as your life. You can have several feelings about the same situation and still recognize those feelings as yours.

Your body has many parts. Your experience contains sensations, memories, thoughts, and changing concerns. You also have relationships with people whose lives continue beyond what you know of them.

Ordinary life already asks us to understand unity and difference together. We speak about one person without assuming that person contains only one thing. We speak about many parts without assuming that their connection is unimportant.

The words *whole* and *part* help us describe those relationships. Their usefulness depends on saying what kind of unity and what kind of participation we mean.

### What I Mean by Whole

A whole is something we can identify as one. Sometimes its unity is simple. Sometimes many distinguishable parts belong together through a particular organization.

A cup has a shape and a function. A word has an arrangement of letters. A conversation has participants and an unfolding exchange. These are different kinds of wholes, and the reasons for treating each as one are different.

Calling something a whole does not settle all those differences. It opens a question about what holds together, how it does so, and what would change if its connections changed.

Take the letters in STOP and POST. The letters are the same. Their arrangement makes them different words. If we list only the letters, we leave out something necessary to explain the word before us.

The connection between parts belongs in our account of the whole.

This is why my framework begins with three considerations: the one, the many, and their connection. We need an account of what the whole is, what is distinguishable within it, and how those distinctions belong together.

A whole can also be unfinished, injured, or in conflict. When I call myself whole, I am identifying the person whose life includes those conditions. I am not claiming perfection or complete control. My wholeness is the subject of the inquiry, including the ways I hold together while changing.

### What I Mean by Part

A part belongs within a wider whole in some identifiable way. We need to name that relationship rather than assume that every form of belonging is identical.

A chapter is part of a book. A person participates in a household. A musician participates in an ensemble. The chapter, the person, and the musician can each be considered in their own right, and each contributes to something beyond themselves.

The musician makes the distinction especially clear. The ensemble depends on how its members play together. Each musician also has a life that the ensemble does not encompass. Being part of this group describes one relationship of a person whose existence extends beyond that role.

A whole can therefore participate as one part without losing all the distinctions that make it a whole.

This relation also helps explain why scale matters. A word can be considered as a whole composed of letters and as a part of a sentence. Looking at the sentence does not make the letters vanish. It changes which relationship we are considering.

Some wholes are collections we establish for a purpose. Three apples can form one group on a table. An organized living body presents a different kind of unity. We should not treat counting a group and explaining a living organization as the same achievement.

Counting shows that one and many are compatible. Explaining a particular whole requires us to go further.

### Body, Mind, and Soul

The most immediate whole in this book is the life each of us calls our own.

I describe mine through three closely related aspects: body, mind, and soul. These names distinguish roles within one life. Their meaning needs to be clear enough that a reader can understand the proposal without already agreeing with it.

My body is my embodied presence and my interface with the world. It is how I encounter things, act, receive effects, and make a difference to what surrounds me. Calling the body an interface emphasizes this participation. The body also has its own internal organization and history.

My mind is the field through which the many aspects of my experience relate. In this framework, mind connects the point of view with the body's parts and with what the person encounters. A sensation can become connected with a memory, a concern, a meaning, or a possible action.

Here, *field* names a proposed organization of relationships. Any further claim that this is a particular physical field would need its own explanation and evidence.

My soul is what I call my singular point of view. It names the proposed unity of the one whose experience this is. The contents of experience are many and changing. The framework asks whether their being experienced together requires an indivisible center, and what such a center could mean.

Using the word *soul* makes that philosophical commitment visible. An account of experience still has work to do before it can establish that the subject is fundamentally indivisible. The word gives us something to examine rather than ending the examination.

When I say, “one whole body, one whole mind, one whole soul,” the word *whole* does different but related work. The body has many organized parts. The mind names a connecting field. The soul names a proposed indivisible unity. Calling all three whole does not require the soul to contain smaller souls.

In this account, consciousness is **centered wholeness**. It is the integration of body, mind, and soul as one experiencing life. That is the framework's proposed account of consciousness, which the later chapters will develop and question.

### The Wholes Within Which I Belong

The opening statement pairs each aspect of my wholeness with a wider belonging.

| Me as whole | Me as part | The relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Body | Earth | My embodied life within the physical world that sustains it. |
| Mind | Greater whole | My inner field of relationships participating in wider relationships. |
| Soul | Reality | My singular point of view within Reality as a whole. |

The first pairing concerns embodiment. My body belongs to the world through which it lives. Its needs and possibilities already place it in relationships beyond itself.

The second concerns connection. What I experience privately takes place amid encounters that other people participate in from their own perspectives. Conversation makes this familiar. We share an exchange while each having a particular experience of it.

The framework proposes that inner and outer relationships belong to a common field, organized differently within and beyond a particular person. Whether a wider organization also has an experience of its own is a further question. Shared participation alone does not answer it.

The third pairing concerns existence itself. My point of view exists within Reality as a whole. This wording matters. It does not mean that my point of view is inside every individual thing. It means that the life from which I experience anything belongs within the all-inclusive reality in which things exist.

In these three pairings, the same “I” is considered through its unity and through its participation.

### A Boundary Gives Relationship a Place

For a particular whole to be distinguishable, there must be some account of what belongs to it and how it relates to what surrounds it.

Sometimes the relevant boundary is physical. Sometimes it concerns a role, a responsibility, or a rule for grouping things. A sentence has a boundary of a different kind from a body. A promise establishes a relationship with limits that cannot be drawn as a line on the ground.

We need to keep these differences visible when using one idea across several situations.

In human life, a boundary can express how we participate. Saying what I can do, what I need, or what I consent to makes my participation clearer. Another person has a perspective and limits of their own. Our relationship takes place through the ways we encounter and respond to each other.

The idea of whole and part helps us notice both participants and the relationship between them. It does not, by itself, decide every conflict or tell us what either person owes the other. For that, we also need reasons about care, fairness, responsibility, and the value we give to people's lives.

Those reasons will matter when the book turns to ethics. An account of what exists can inform our responsibilities, but the values in that argument should be stated openly.

### Everything and Every Thing

The title contains a distinction that deserves attention from the beginning.

*Every thing* directs our attention to particular things. We can ask what makes each one identifiable and how it participates in a wider reality.

*Everything* directs our attention to Reality considered as an all-inclusive whole. Its differences and relationships are within it. If it includes everything that exists, we should not casually place another existing whole outside it.

The book therefore has two related tasks. One is to investigate particular wholes and their participation. The other is to investigate the claim that they belong within one Reality.

This prevents the phrase “whole within a greater whole” from becoming an unexplained demand for an endless stack. Many relationships can be nested without our having established that every nesting continues forever. Reality, when defined as all-inclusive, needs to be treated as that limiting case.

Likewise, describing Reality as one does not tell us whether it has one point of view. Its unity by inclusion and a person's proposed unity of perspective are different meanings of *one*.

The title announces an inquiry into these relationships. A full argument must account for the differences between being identifiable, having parts, belonging to a collection, and participating in an organized whole. A familiar example can show that a relationship is possible. Extending it to every case calls for further reasons.

### Beginning with a Life

We can begin before those larger questions are settled.

I have a life I recognize as my own. That life includes many aspects, some easier to acknowledge than others. It also takes place among other lives and within a world whose conditions I did not create alone.

Attending to one part can help me understand something important. Attending to the wider whole can reveal relationships that the narrower focus leaves out. Both kinds of attention have a place.

The experience that opened this book gave this distinction a personal use. When the surge in my chest occupied my attention, remembering my whole body helped me include more of myself. That use does not prove an ontology. It shows why I want to investigate one.

I want an account that can make sense of having a center without losing connection, of belonging without losing distinction, and of changing while remaining the person whose life is changing.

To develop that account, we need to look more closely at the third term: the connection between the one and the many.

## Interlude: Structure to Flow Within

I had spent hours in virtual reality editing 3D models. Close up, I could see thousands of points and the lines connecting them. I could move a point, and when it was connected to others in the way I was working, they moved together as a web. A change in one place could change the larger form.

The feeling afterward was like stepping off a boat after a day on the water and still feeling its movement. After those hours in VR, the sense of the grid and its connections lingered in a similar way.

When I closed my eyes, I imagined the inside of my body as that kind of three-dimensional grid. It felt calming.

I had an image of many parts remaining connected while they moved. It gave a felt shape to the thought that had already been helping me: I am a whole, made of parts, and part of a greater whole.

The phrase I use for this is **structure to flow within**.

I am describing an experience of imagination and embodiment. Its calming effect belongs to my account of what happened. The further question is what that image helps me notice about how I live.

### A Center in a Field

Another image came through thinking about a cosmic system. The center of my attention is like a black hole. My body is the suns and planets. My mind is the field connecting them.

The image brings together a center, an embodied whole, and a field of relationships. The black hole represents inward convergence: attention gathering toward a focus. The surrounding system lets me picture the many parts of a whole whose relationships extend beyond that focus.

I call the singular point of view soul. I understand mind as the field around that center, relating what I experience through my body. The circumpunct, a point within a circle, gives this proposal a compact form. Its point names the center, its interior the field, and its boundary the bodily interface through which the whole participates.

In the cosmic image, attention can gather at one point while awareness remains open to the larger system. In the grid image, a part can move while its connections remain. Both images help me explore what I mean by centered wholeness.

### Attention Across Scales

I can consider my body as one whole, then turn toward its organs and tissues, cells and organelles, molecules, atoms, and quantum processes. Each shift changes which relationships I am attending to. Imagining a scale does not give me direct sensory access to everything occurring there, but it can remind me that my life depends on parts I cannot individually perceive.

My proposal is that the relationship between center and field continues through the scales of embodied participation. At whatever scale the center engages with the physical, mind remains its surrounding field of relationship. This is what I mean when I say mind permeates the physical at each scale of engagement. Explaining how that relation could operate physically remains part of the framework's work.

The claim concerns one point of view and the scales through which its life participates. It does not place my point of view separately inside every individual thing I consider.

I also propose a wider possibility: physical composition continues without a final layer. Even apparent emptiness contains further physical reality. On this account, space itself participates in composition. Every apparent gap has further structure, and that structure has further parts.

I call this **infinitely finite**: finite forms within an endless depth of relationships, one and many, whole and part. Composition everywhere and composition without end are two aspects of the universal premise I am proposing. Within that proposal, even the Planck scale would not be reality's final layer.

This extends beyond what the familiar examples have established. The existence of many known scales does not establish an endless hierarchy. The hypothesis needs arguments and, where it makes physical claims, evidence. Its relation to the proposed indivisibility of a point of view also needs explanation. Endless composition within Reality would not require placing another whole outside Reality as the all-inclusive whole.

Those questions remain open. Honouring the parts that sustain my life can begin with the relationships I already have reason to recognize.

### A Boundary That Supports Movement

Sometimes I experience my center as being pulled about by the gravity of people, things, demands, and events. My attention follows what is urgent or emotionally powerful. I can have difficulty keeping contact with my own needs and choices within that pull.

A clear boundary gives my participation a shape. I can ask what I am feeling, what matters to me, what I can take on, and what I consent to. Another person's experience matters. My experience matters too.

The aperture is the adjustable opening in that boundary. It concerns how I engage, how much I take in, how quickly I respond, and when I need space. Through it I can explore staying connected to myself while remaining in contact with the world.

Structure and flow belong together here. Structure gives movement coherence. Flow allows adjustment as circumstances change. Bruce Lee's image of water, capable of flowing and crashing, helps me express the range: softness and force, yielding and holding, finding a passage and meeting resistance.

The practical question is what response fits the situation. A firm boundary can be expressed gently. A flexible response can carry conviction. My values provide continuity while I reconsider how to act.

I can struggle with this and remain whole. A surge of feeling does not make me responsible for having failed to control every part of myself. What I am developing is a way to notice my participation and the choices available within it.

### An Opening for Correction

The boundary also matters when someone challenges me.

I can become invested in an image of myself. A criticism may then seem to threaten the person I believe I am. Protecting that image can take priority over examining what happened, hearing another person's experience, or acknowledging harm.

When I speak of narcissistic defensiveness in this context, I mean the pattern of protecting an image at the expense of truth. The ethical questions concern what a person does: whether they listen, give reasons, accept responsibility, and change their conduct. A person's point of view remains present even when their response is defensive or dishonest.

In the language of the aperture, openness around the center can narrow. Calling this closure of the soul expresses a concern about how a person relates to truth and to others. Establishing a literal change in the soul itself would require a further account. The label applied to a person cannot settle that question.

**Corrigibility is the willingness to remain open to correction.**

**Corrigibility takes courage.** Being wrong can sting. A correction may challenge a belief I care about, an image I have of myself, or a choice whose consequences I must face. Courage is the willingness to stay with that discomfort long enough to listen, examine the reasons, and learn.

I can be mistaken and remain worthy of respect. I can acknowledge that I caused harm and take responsibility for what follows. Correction can require a change in my understanding, a change in my behaviour, or both.

That courage can take the form of admitting an error, asking a question, making an apology, or trying again with a better understanding. I can allow the feeling of being wrong to move through me while giving what is true a chance to change what I do.

This openness requires discernment. I can ask for reasons, examine evidence, consider another perspective, and accept a valid correction. I can also challenge an accusation that is mistaken or unfair. My judgment participates throughout. A boundary can support both my willingness to learn and my ability to say no.

The same responsibility applies to this book. My ideas about soul, mind, matter, and scale must remain answerable to evidence and reasoning. If protecting the framework becomes more important than examining it, I would be repeating the pattern I am questioning.

Corrigibility gives the framework a continuing task: make its commitments clear enough that something could count as a reason to revise them.

### Returning to the Whole

I honour the parts that sustain my conscious life, including the parts outside my present awareness. Their importance continues wherever my attention goes.

I also care for myself as the person whose life includes those parts and their relationships. Beyond me are other people, each with an experience and needs of their own, and the physical world through which our lives are possible.

My ethical commitments enter here: people's experience, agency, and welfare matter. The distinctions between whole and part help me consider how an action affects a person, their relationships, and the wider conditions we share. I still need reasons for what I choose to do.

The practice returns me to three directions of attention: inward toward my parts, toward myself as one whole, and outward toward the greater whole I participate in. I can move among them while remembering that each is one way of attending to the same life.

> I am a whole, made of parts, and part of a greater whole.
>
> I remain centered as I turn my attention inward toward my parts, hold awareness of myself as one whole, and open outward toward the greater whole I participate in.
>
> I create enough structure to let myself flow, and enough openness to let myself learn.

## Chapter 2: The One, the Many, and the Connection

Chapter 1 ended with the third term: the connection between the one and the many. Before we can look at the connection, we need to see why the first two terms do not compete. Then we need to see how little that settles.

The thought behind the title is older than the framework. I first put it into words when I was eleven: “Everything is wholes and parts.” **[AUTHOR: confirm wording and age; docs/wholes-parts.html attributes this line to you at eleven, while other files date an age-eleven insight to 1993 or 1995 in different words]** The sentence is simple. What it needs before anyone can reasonably believe it is not.

I was not the first to notice what it names. Arthur Koestler coined the word *holon* for anything that is at once a whole in its own right and a part of something larger. **[SOURCE: verify Koestler, *The Ghost in the Machine* (1967), on the holon]** A name marks a relationship. It does not yet explain it. We still need to say what makes something one, and what kind of belonging makes it a part.

### One Deck, Fifty-Two Cards

Put a deck of cards on the table and ask how many there are.

The question cannot be answered yet. How many what? If you mean decks, there is one. If you mean cards, there are fifty-two. If you mean suits, there are four. Each answer is correct, and none of them corrects the others.

Gottlob Frege made this point about number. The same pile of cards can be counted as cards or as packs, and what changes between the answers is not the cards but the concept under which we count them. **[SOURCE: verify Frege, *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884), §22 and §46; paraphrase, not quotation]**

Two answers are rivals only when they answer the same question. “One deck” and “three decks” are rivals. “One deck” and “fifty-two cards” are not. They answer different questions about the same cards.

**One and many are not rivals. They answer different counting questions.**

The same holds for whole and part, as far as counting can take us. Counted as decks, the deck is one whole. Counted as cards, each card is one of its fifty-two parts. Each card can also be counted on its own: one card, with a face and a back. Counted another way again, it is many: layers of paper and ink. The same card is whole and part at once, under different ways of counting, and nothing in that is contradictory.

This is what the counting argument establishes: compatibility. It removes an objection before the inquiry begins, the objection that one and many, or whole and part, must exclude each other. The opening statement of this book says “I am whole” and “I am part.” Counting shows that the two lines can both be true. It does not yet show that they are.

### What Counting Cannot Tell Us

Counting works on any group we care to name. The moon, my left shoe, and the last word of this sentence can be counted as one odd trio or as three things. Both counts are correct. Nobody thinks the moon and my shoe have become one thing because I counted them together.

So counting cannot tell a grouping we have merely chosen from a thing whose parts hold together on their own. A pile and a living body can each be counted as one. So can a crowd and a community. The arithmetic is the same in each case. What differs is something counting does not look at.

Counting also presupposes something. To count cards, each card has to be set apart from the others and from the table. There must be an edge, or a rule, that says where one ends and the next begins. That is already a kind of oneness, and it is the thinnest kind. Sand in a bag is one bagful, and the sand has not become anything.

Philosophers have a name for the question counting leaves open. Peter van Inwagen called it the special composition question: when do many things together make up one thing? **[SOURCE: verify van Inwagen, *Material Beings* (1990), on the special composition question and his own answer]** Some philosophers answer that it always happens: any things whatever make up something. Others answer that it never does: only things without parts truly exist. Van Inwagen's own answer was that many things make up one only when their activity makes up a life.

This chapter does not need to choose among those answers. A bag of sand and a living body are both one in some sense. The useful question is in what sense, and that question has answers we can investigate.

To describe actual things, then, we need further premises, and they are not about counting. What holds the parts together, if anything does besides our counting them? How do they hold: in what arrangement, and in what relationships? And does the whole take any part in its own holding? The answers differ from case to case. Those differences are what the rest of this chapter is about.

### Arrangement

The first further premise is arrangement: the order and position of the parts relative to one another.

Chapter 1 used STOP and POST. Aristotle made a related point with syllables. He distinguished things that are one like a heap from things that are one like a syllable. A syllable is not merely its letters, he argued, because when it is broken up the letters remain and the syllable does not. Something besides the letters makes it one. **[SOURCE: verify Aristotle, *Metaphysics* VII.17, 1041b; paraphrase]**

This is why a list of parts is not yet an account of a whole. A list records what is present. It leaves out where each part stands in relation to the others, and the same list can describe different wholes.

Arrangement is necessary. It is not always enough. Consider a castle a child builds from plastic bricks. The bricks sit in a definite order, they grip one another, and the castle has a shape anyone would recognize. We call it one castle, and we are right to.

Now knock a tower off. The rest of the castle stands as it stood. Nothing in it responds to the loss, closes the gap, or carries on differently. The castle's unity is its shape, the grip of brick on brick, and the name we give it.

That is a real unity and a modest one. Arrangement explains why the castle is a castle and not a pile. It does not make what each part does depend on the rest, and it gives the castle no part in keeping itself whole.

### Relationship

The second further premise is relationship: the ways the parts act on and depend on one another.

Imagine a pocket watch taken apart and laid out on a cloth. The gears lie in a neat row, the spring beside them, the face and hands at the end. Nothing has been lost or broken. Every part of the watch is on the cloth, and there is no watch.

The parts are still arranged, and neatly. What has gone is the particular arrangement that put them into working relationship: the spring driving the gears, the gears turning the hands, a change in one reaching all the others. A heap is parts near each other. A working whole is parts made mutually relevant.

A useful sign of that difference, though not a definition, is what happens when one part is removed. Take a grain from a heap and nothing else changes. Take a gear from a working watch and every other part changes its status at once, from component to loose piece, without being touched. In a whole of this kind, a loss registers.

The sign needs care, because living things survive many losses. A body can lose blood, skin, even a limb, and go on. That is not because the losses fail to register. It is because a living body responds to them. A cut bleeds, closes, and heals.

That response points to a further difference. A stone stays together through conditions it does nothing to maintain. A living body takes part in holding itself together: it eats and breathes, repairs damage, shivers in the cold and sweats in the heat. Nothing holds itself together entirely alone, and Chapter 7 looks at how much a body depends on what it did not make. Still, some wholes are only held, and some also hold.

A sum tells us what is present. A whole tells us how what is present belongs together. Arrangement and relationship are the two halves of that “how.” An account that leaves out either one cannot explain the whole in front of it.

### Four Ways to Belong

Chapter 1 listed what a full argument must keep apart: being identifiable, having parts, belonging to a collection, and participating in an organized whole. Each points to a different relation, and each answers a different question.

*Identity* is being one and the same thing. Its question is: is this the same as that? “The card in my hand” and “the queen of hearts” can be two descriptions of one card. Identity is what we track when we pick something out as this one and not another. It is not a kind of parthood. I can lose a tooth and remain myself, and the tooth was never me.

*Composition* is the relation of a thing to what it is made of. Its question is: what is it made of? Cards compose a deck; cells compose a body. Take every constituent away and nothing of the thing remains. Composition always comes with an arrangement and with relationships, which is why the last two sections were needed.

*Membership* is belonging by meeting a rule. Its question is: does it qualify? The thirteen hearts are members of their suit wherever they lie, touching or not. A person can belong to a club on paper and never attend. Membership requires no contact, no contribution, and no shared activity.

*Participation* is taking part: contributing to something ongoing and being affected by it, while remaining oneself. Its question is: what does it do there, and what is done to it? The musician of Chapter 1 participates in an ensemble. Participation comes in degrees, and it can happen without membership, as when a guest joins the singing at a party.

These four come apart in ordinary life. A card helps compose a deck and takes part in nothing until it is dealt. A lapsed member belongs to a club without taking part in it. A guest takes part without belonging. And none of these relations is identity: a member is not the club, and the guest is not the party.

Confusing them produces two characteristic failures, which later chapters take up. When participation is mistaken for identity, we get a part standing for the whole: a role taken for the person, or one group claiming to be everything. When participation is reduced to composition or membership, we get a whole reduced to a part: a person treated as a function, an entry on a list, a component with no life of its own.

When the opening statement says “I am part,” the relation I mean is participation. In the body's case, composition belongs to it too: the food and water I take in become my body, and my body returns material to the world. I do not mean membership on a list, and I do not mean identity. I am not Earth. **[AUTHOR: confirm that participation, with composition in the body's case, is the sense of “part” you intend in the opening statement]** The third line, “One part of Reality,” needs its own account, which Chapter 9 gives.

### Kinds of Oneness

The word *one* needs the same sorting. Something can be one in several ways, and each way gives a criterion we can check.

A thing is one by *distinction* when an edge or a rule sets it apart from what it is not. A cup is one in this way, and so is a bag of sand. This is the oneness counting presupposes.

A thing is one by *continuity* when its moments stay connected, each leading into the next. A conversation is one this way. So is a river that keeps its name while its water changes.

A thing is one by *coherence* when many relations belong to one interior, so that each part is shaped by the condition of the whole and not only by what touches it. The framework proposes that a mind is one this way. A room of people can share a mood so fully that a gesture means something there it would mean nowhere else. Whether the room is anyone, with a point of view of its own, is a separate question, and coherence alone does not answer it.

A thing is one by *integration* when its many parts answer to one center. This is more than spokes attached to a hub. The parts also bear on one another, and all of it matters to the same one. This is the oneness the framework proposes for a person.

A thing is one by *indivisibility* when there is nothing in it to cut. A geometric point is one this way. The framework proposes that the center of a life is too, a claim Chapter 3 examines.

Finally, there is oneness by *inclusion*: nothing lies outside it. In Chapter 1, this was the oneness of Reality.

The kinds combine, and they come apart. A bag of sand is one by distinction and little else. A working watch adds the working relations of its parts. A person, Chapter 3 proposes, is one in five of these ways at once.

Used as criteria, the kinds turn a vague question into definite ones. Instead of asking whether something is really one, we can ask: Is there an edge? Do its moments connect? Do its relations share an interior? Do its parts answer to a center? A collection, a working mechanism, and a conscious person are not interchangeable examples, and these questions show why.

Many confusions about oneness mistake one kind for another. A family or a movement that demands everyone think alike has mistaken sameness for unity: integration keeps its parts distinct, and sameness erases them. A relationship that requires one person to disappear into the other has enforced belonging as merger. And the thought that, because everything is included in Reality, Reality must be one the way a person is one, with a point of view of its own, mistakes inclusion for integration. Chapter 1 cautioned against that step, and Chapter 9 makes the argument. **[RULING R1: Reality is one by inclusion, not a centered whole with a point of view of its own]**

### Two Senses of Whole

Chapter 1 used *whole* in a broad sense: anything we can identify as one. In that sense a cup, a word, a conversation, and three apples on a table are all wholes. I want to keep that sense. It matches ordinary speech, and it lets the title speak of every thing.

The kinds of oneness show why a strict sense is needed beside it. Some wholes are one by distinction or arrangement and little more. Others are one by integration. **A centered whole is many parts integrated around one center.** That is the strict sense, and it is the sense the book's central proposals concern.

None of this makes the broad wholes unreal. A deck is really one deck, and a castle really one castle. The kinds of oneness do not sort things into real and unreal. They say how each thing is one.

The difference matters most for consciousness. In Chapter 1 I proposed that consciousness is centered wholeness. That proposal concerns centered wholes only. It does not suggest that a cup, as a cup, has an inner life, or that a bag of sand experiences anything. **[RULING R2: “whole” in the broad sense, “centered whole” in the strict sense; the account of consciousness applies only to the strict sense]**

Which things are centered wholes? A person is the case this book develops. Others are harder. A family, a nation, and Earth each have something like a boundary, relationships within, and continuity through time. The open question is the center.

The framework offers a test for it: is there one indivisible center, or a center-shaped region occupied by several? A team's shared goals sit where a center would be, and they can be dropped, renegotiated, or added while the team carries on. Nothing can be taken from a point. I offer this as a test, not a verdict. Chapter 8 applies it to groups and to the wider relationships in which a mind takes part.

The two senses guard against opposite mistakes. Without the broad sense, we would have no words for the many things that are one without being anyone. Without the strict sense, we might credit every identifiable unit with a point of view, or treat a person as if they were one only in the way a cup is one.

The clearest candidate for a centered whole is the one we know from inside. Chapter 1 called it the most immediate whole in this book: the life each of us calls our own. What kind of whole is that? What plays the part of its center, its boundary, and its interior, and what would it take for many parts to make one experiencing life? Those are the questions of Chapter 3.

## Chapter 3: Body, Mind, and Soul

My mother told me that my first persistent question was “Ossat?” It was my way of asking “What is that?” I pointed at things and wanted them named: an object, a sound, an animal, a person. **[AUTHOR: confirm use of the “Ossat?” story here, and of the summary sentence from it quoted later in this chapter; the story also opens *Redefining Reality*]**

Eventually the question expanded. I began asking what *this* is: this experience, this world, all of this taken together. *That* points away from the one asking. *This* includes the one asking.

Chapter 2 was mostly about *that*: decks, castles, watches, and the different ways each is one. This chapter turns to *this*: the life from which any asking is done. Chapter 1 described that life through body, mind, and soul. Here I develop them as an anatomy, add an element Chapter 1 left implicit, and ask what would make such an organization one experiencing life. I also want to state plainly what the proposal costs.

### Center, Field, Boundary, Setting

The circumpunct is drawn with two marks: a point and a circle around it. The point names the center, the circle the boundary, and the space between them the field. Outside the circle lies everything the life is not.

The center is what I call the soul: the singular point of view, the one whose experience this is. It is not one of the things experienced. It is where experiencing is done from. The framework proposes that it is indivisible, a claim this chapter examines.

The field is what I call the mind: the interior in which a life's many aspects relate. A sensation meets a memory there, a memory meets a concern, a concern turns toward an action. *Field* names what mind is like. As for how mind comes to be, the proposal is that it arises within the inside that the body's closure gives a life. **[RULING R8: mind as field; it arises within the inside the body's closure provides]**

The boundary is the body in its role as interface: how a life encounters the world and is encountered by it. It is one surface with two names. From within, it is the edge of me. From without, it is where the world is already touching me. The skin that holds me in is also what light, warmth, and another person's hand arrive at.

Beyond the boundary is the wider setting: other lives, Earth, and the rest of Reality, within which the whole life takes place. Chapter 1's three pairings belong here, and Part III returns to them.

The drawing leaves one thing out, because a drawing is a single moment. Draw the same figure an instant later and the center has traced a line. That line is continuity: the thread that keeps a changing life one over time, carried in memory, in commitments, and in the history a life accumulates. **[RULING R9: continuity joins the anatomy; the opening statement stays body, mind, soul]**

In another essay I put the whole anatomy into one sentence: my consciousness is a center with no size, surrounded by a field and articulated through a boundary, and through time the center traces a line of continuity. **[AUTHOR: confirm this plain rendering of your sentence in the ghost dragon essays]** In Chapter 2's terms, the center is one by indivisibility, the thread by continuity, the body by distinction, the mind by coherence, and the life as a whole by integration.

These are roles, not measurements. My body is not a thin surface. It has depth, organs, and a history of its own, and it plays the part of boundary because it is where my life meets what is not my life. Geometry supplies the image: a circle on paper has a one-dimensional edge around a two-dimensional inside, and a ball has a two-dimensional surface around a three-dimensional volume. The image is not a measurement of a person. Nor is the whole a further piece of the drawing. Integrating the parts adds no new region; it is the drawing taken as one. **[RULING R7: center, thread, boundary, and field as roles, not literal dimensions]**

### Two Orders

The anatomy can be read in two orders, and they disagree in one place.

The first is the order in which a whole forms. A center gathers. Its holding over time draws a thread. The thread closes into a boundary, and within the closure a field forms. The step that carries the weight is the closing. There is no inside until something has closed around one, and a field needs an inside to be the field of.

This is an order of dependence, not a timetable. It says what requires what. It does not say when, in the growth of a body, a point of view begins, a question this book does not settle.

The second is the order in which a formed life is met from its center outward: center, then field, then boundary, then world. Mind lies between the point of view and the body. Experience runs from the center through the field to the boundary, and the world arrives by the same route in reverse.

The two orders swap the field and the boundary, and neither is wrong. Walk outward from the center of a ball and you pass through its volume before you reach its surface. A surface is thinner than what it encloses, and it is reached later. Thinness and lateness are different properties. A boundary comes before the field in the order of forming and after it in the order of meeting.

The order of meeting also runs as a loop. The world changes the body, the body informs the mind, and the mind brings experience to its center. The center's condition moves back out through the mind into the body and into action. The loop has no absolute starting point. I describe it from the center because that is where the describing is done from. Each turn of it becomes part of the thread.

### Mind and Consciousness

It is common to treat mind and consciousness as one thing. In this framework they have different roles.

Mind is the field, one aspect of a life. Consciousness, as Chapter 1 proposed, is centered wholeness: the whole life (center, thread, boundary, and field) integrated and lived from its center. It is not located in any one of these alone.

One way to see why is to imagine each element missing. A field without a center would be relations held together and experienced from nowhere. A center without a field would be a point with nothing related to it. Without a boundary there would be no inside and no contact. Without continuity there would be a string of flashes rather than someone. The center gives experience a point of view, the thread gives it continuity, the boundary gives it distinction and contact, and the field gives it an interior where things can relate.

When I wrote down the Ossat story, I summarized the framework's answer this way: there is infinite Reality, there are indivisible centers of experience, and each center meets Reality from a particular point of view. “Mind mediates that encounter, body gives it an interface, and the continuing relationship becomes a conscious life.”

On this proposal, consciousness is not something a whole produces, the way an engine produces exhaust. **It is what the whole is, from within.** Consider a house. Its walls do not give off an inside as a second product. Having walls closed in that way is what it is to have an inside, and no one finds the inside by studying the bricks from the street. To find it, you go in. A life has the same two faces. Instruments and other people meet its outside. Its inside is what being it is like.

This is also why no list of a person's parts includes their experience. A list holds its items side by side. A life is one by integration: its parts are gathered around a center. Asking which item on the list is the experience is like asking which brick is the inside of the house.

I want to be exact about what this does. It says where experience is and why it never appears among the parts. It does not explain why there is anything it is like to be such a whole. The framework takes that as its starting point rather than deriving it, and a reader is entitled to ask more of it.

### One Point of View

The proposal about the soul has two parts: that a life is lived from one point of view, and that this point of view is indivisible. Three considerations support the first.

The first is a search. David Hume reported that whenever he looked inward for his self, he found only some particular perception: heat or cold, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. He never caught himself without a perception, and never observed anything but the perception. **[SOURCE: verify Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), Book 1, Part 4, Section 6; paraphrase]** His report is accurate, and its shape is telling. Every item he found was found by being presented to the one searching. The eye never appears in its own visual field, because it is what the field is seen from. A point of view would not turn up among its own contents.

The second is togetherness. When you hear a sentence, you hear the sentence, not a scatter of words. William James asked us to give each of twelve people one word of a sentence: however hard each thinks of their word, nobody hears the sentence. **[SOURCE: verify James, *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), ch. 6; paraphrase]** Contents experienced together seem to need one for whom they are together.

The third is a count. A person can describe many selves: one at work, one at home, an angry one, a patient one. When a hammer misses the nail and lands on a thumb, how many of them are hurt? The roles are many, and the one who hurts is one. Even the sentence “I am many selves” is spoken by one I.

It helps to keep three things apart. The *image* is the picture of me, in my head and in other people's: fixed traits, a reputation, a story. It is a construction, and the skeptics are right that it is assembled and often wrong. The *self* is the whole: body, mind, and soul together, real and changing. As I have put it before, “The self is not an illusion either. It's the whole. Yes it changes, but it's not an illusion.” **[AUTHOR: confirm quotation and its use here; recorded in plans/one_book_charter.md (v1.9, 2026-08-09) as your correction in the “many selves” exchange]** To conclude that the self is unreal because it changes requires a premise nobody defends aloud: that only unchanging things are real. A river changes its water and is not an illusion. The *center* is the third thing: the point of view the whole is lived from.

### What the Proposal Needs

The considerations above support two claims: that an experience has one subject at a time, and that this subject is not one of its contents. They do not establish that the subject is indivisible. An experience's apparent unity is not enough for that.

Indivisibility needs a further premise, and the framework supplies it as a proposal. The point of view is not an extended thing with parts. It is the position a whole is lived from, and a position cannot be halved. Half a point of view would not be a smaller point of view. It would be two. If this premise is right, points of view come in whole numbers.

That gives the proposal something to answer to. The sharpest challenge comes from people whose brain hemispheres have been surgically disconnected. Under special test conditions, some act as though each side knows what the other does not. **[SOURCE: the split-brain studies of Roger Sperry, Michael Gazzaniga, and colleagues]** Philosophers disagree about what follows. Thomas Nagel argued that there may be no whole number of minds in such cases. **[SOURCE: verify Nagel, “Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness,” *Synthese* (1971)]** Tim Bayne has defended the view that consciousness remains unified. **[SOURCE: verify Bayne, *The Unity of Consciousness* (2010), on split-brain cases]** If Nagel is right, the proposal is in trouble. The debate is genuine, and evidence is hard to gather, because from outside, experience can be counted only through behaviour and brain activity, never directly.

A second challenge would be an explanation of experience's unity that needs no indivisible center at all. Many scientific accounts aim to explain unity by how processes in the brain are integrated or shared. **[SOURCE: e.g., global workspace theory (Baars; Dehaene); integrated information theory (Tononi)]** If such an account explained everything the center is invoked to explain, the further premise would be idle. The framework's reply is that these accounts explain how contents are bound together and leave open for whom they are bound. That reply is a judgment, not a proof.

The same caution runs the other way. Coordination among parts does not by itself establish consciousness. Traffic lights can be timed to work together across a city, and nothing in that requires a point of view belonging to the system. The proposal is not that coordination produces experience. It is that a centered whole of the kind described has an inside.

### Criteria for One Experiencing Whole

From the anatomy, the framework proposes four criteria for calling an organization one experiencing whole.

*One center*: a single point of view, rather than a center-shaped region occupied by several. *A boundary that gives an inside*: something closes around the life, so that there is an inside and an outside, and exchange between them is regulated. *An interior field*: many relations held within that inside, each affected by the condition of the whole. *Continuity*: the center persists and traces a thread through time. Continuity here means that the one who experiences persists, not that every moment is remembered.

The four must also be integrated, their parts answering to the one center, which is the strict sense of *whole* from Chapter 2. Whether an organization has an inside at all, on this proposal, depends on whether the closure happens. How rich that inside is depends on how much is integrated within it.

These are proposed criteria, not established tests. Several can be checked from outside: boundaries, organization, continuity. The center cannot be observed directly, which is why the hard cases are hard. Chapter 8 applies the criteria to groups and coordinated systems, and Chapter 12 collects the cases and the evidence that would bear on them.

The proposal has a cost, and I want to state it plainly. If consciousness is centered wholeness, then any centered whole has some inside, however simple. The framework proposes that many things far smaller than a person, perhaps living cells, meet these criteria in a simple form. If they do, each has an inside of its own, unimaginably simpler than ours. That places the proposal near panpsychism, the view that experience is widespread in nature. **[SOURCE: e.g., Goff, *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017); Chalmers on panpsychism]** I accept the cost openly rather than hide it. It is not the claim that everything experiences. A heap of sand has no center and no closure, and a cup, as a cup, has no center its parts answer to. **[RULING R2: the account of consciousness applies only to centered wholes, not to every whole in the broad sense]**

The cost brings a hard question with it. If my cells have insides, how do their insides relate to mine? Philosophers call this the combination problem. **[SOURCE: verify Chalmers, “The Combination Problem for Panpsychism” (2017)]** James's twelve people sharpen it: many small experiences placed side by side do not make one larger one. The framework's answer is that they do not add up at all. A person is not a crowd of cell experiences gathered in one body. A person is one integration around one center. Whether that answer works in detail is an open question.

### Across the Scales of a Life

In the interlude I proposed that the relation between center and field continues through the scales of embodied participation. Here is what that means, and how far the framework can presently take it.

A life engages the physical at many scales at once. When I lift a cup, the movement happens at the scale of my body in a room. Muscles work at the scale of tissue. Cells use fuel. Chemistry takes place. It is one action, engaged at all of those scales together. To engage at a scale, in this sense, is to make a difference there and to be affected by what happens there. A choice of mine changes what happens in the chemistry of my muscles. Hunger, which begins in processes I cannot perceive one by one, can arrive at the center as irritability.

The proposal is that at each scale where a life engages, mind is the field relating what happens there to the center. It is not that my point of view sits inside each cell. A cell that takes part in my body keeps its own organization. From my scale it is one participant among very many, and that does not reduce it to a mere point. The same is true of me in a household: one member from above, a whole life from within.

I should mark a gap honestly. The framework so far supports one reading of this better than another. It fits well with the reading that each scale has its own centered wholes, each organized from within, and that my life relates to them through their participation. It supports less well the stronger reading, that one center's field reaches down through every scale of the body. Any account agrees that events at small scales affect a life and that a life affects them. To say more than that, the proposal needs to explain how mind relates to what happens at each scale, and what observation would show the difference. That work remains open. **[AUTHOR: which reading do you intend by “mind permeates the physical at each scale of engagement”: one center's field reaching down, or a field relating many centered wholes at their own scales?]**

The body is where those scales meet the world, and where one life meets others. What crosses a boundary, who decides, and how a life can belong to a relationship without being absorbed by it are the questions of Chapter 4.

# Part II: Living at the Boundary

## Chapter 4: Boundaries and Participation

A life meets other lives at the body. Before I know what someone thinks, I have heard their voice and seen their face. Before they know what I think, they have met my posture, my tone, and the distance I keep.

In Chapter 1 I said that a boundary gives relationship a place. This chapter looks at that place: what crosses it, what limits people live by, what consent requires, and how a person can belong to a relationship while keeping a perspective and a life of their own.

### Meeting and Being Met

My body is how I encounter other people and how they encounter me. The exchange runs both ways. I hear a friend's voice, and my voice reaches them. A hand on my shoulder is something I feel, and my shoulder is something the other person touches. My face shows more than I intend, and I read more in another face than its owner says.

The plainer traffic of a life crosses the same edge. Air, water, and food come in every day; warmth and breath go out. Chapter 7 returns to that dependence. Here the subject is the meeting of lives.

A boundary that let nothing cross would not be a living body. Nothing could meet it, and it could meet nothing. A boundary that let everything cross would no longer mark where one life ends and another begins. A living body does neither. It selects.

I will call that selecting **the body's gate**: the boundary's regulation of what enters and leaves, how much, how fast, and in what form.

Some of this regulation happens without deliberation. Eyes narrow in bright light. We flinch before we know why. Some of it is chosen. We lean in to listen, step back from someone standing too close, end a conversation, or ask for a slower pace.

In Chapter 2's terms, a body is one by distinction, set apart from what it is not. The gate shows that distinction is not separation: the body is set apart, and it takes part.

### Different Kinds of Limit

Chapter 1 noted that a promise has limits that cannot be drawn as a line on the ground. The word *boundary* covers very different things. Consider four: a skin, a promise, a role, and a rule.

A skin is a physical boundary. It exists because of what a body is, and it does its work whether or not anyone acknowledges it. It can be touched, warmed, or pierced.

A promise is a limit I place on my own future conduct. It exists because I gave my word to someone. Nothing physical marks it, and it can be broken without anyone being touched.

A role marks the scope of what may be asked of someone in a particular capacity. A teacher may ask a student for an essay and may not ask for a personal favour. A rule for grouping marks who belongs to a team, a club, or a jurisdiction; Chapter 2 distinguished that kind of membership from participation.

The promise and the role have something in common with the limits people state in their relationships. “Please don't call me after ten.” “I'm not willing to discuss my health at work.” “Ask before you borrow my things.”

I will call all of these *ethical boundaries*. They exist by being given or stated and understood, and they hold when they are respected. Words alone can cross them, and crossing one is a wrong done to a person, not only a contact between bodies.

Keeping the kinds distinct prevents two confusions. The first treats physical contact as the only real crossing: “I never laid a hand on you.” The absence of contact does not show that no limit was crossed.

The second treats a personal limit as if it were visible like skin. Some limits are so widely shared that no one needs to say them. Many personal ones are not, and saying them is part of how they come to hold between people. That does not make an unspoken limit fair game, and it does not make a person who could not speak responsible for what was done to them.

The two kinds also meet: the body often registers a crossing before a person can name the limit, and each can inform the other.

### Consent

The boundary is where consent becomes possible. Consent needs two centers, each able to say yes and each able to say no.

The working definition I use is this: **consent means that the effects of what crosses my boundary are within what I understand and accept.** **[AUTHOR: confirm this working definition of consent, taken from your ethics and virtues page.]** It has two parts. I understand what I am agreeing to well enough that its effects do not ambush me. And I accept it, in a situation where I could have refused.

The second part carries the weight. Where no genuine refusal is possible, agreement has not occurred.

Compliance may have occurred. Submission may have occurred. Fear may have produced something that looks like agreement from the outside. None of these is consent. Medical ethics reaches a similar standard by its own route, treating informed consent as requiring both understanding and voluntariness. **[SOURCE: verify, e.g., Faden and Beauchamp, *A History and Theory of Informed Consent* (1986), or Beauchamp and Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*]**

It follows that no one can be believed into consenting. I can imagine a relationship with someone, hope for it, and offer it. I cannot complete it alone. Their yes has to come from where they stand, not from my picture of them.

Consent also belongs to the present. A friendship or a partnership can establish that certain things may be asked. It does not answer each request in advance. A relationship makes the asking possible; consent is the answer, given now. A yes given last year is a fact about last year, and a yes can be taken back.

Why does consent matter so much? The structure just described says where consent happens and what makes it genuine. It does not say what anyone owes.

For that I state a value openly: people's experience, agency, and welfare matter. Combine that value with the plain fact that no one can live from another person's point of view, and consent becomes a requirement: what happens to a person should, as far as possible, pass through that person's own judgment. The framework proposes something further, that such values are not arbitrary because they track the conditions under which wholes stay whole. Chapter 11 examines that proposal; the argument here does not depend on it. **[RULING R4: values stated as premises, with consent following from them plus the structure; the claim that ethics follows from wholeness appears only as a further proposal.]**

### Two Openings

In the interlude I called the aperture “the adjustable opening in that boundary.” I want to separate two things that sentence held together, because they behave differently and fail differently.

The first is the body's gate. It faces the world. It concerns what crosses, when, how fast, and in what form: how much I take in, how quickly I respond, when I need space. Everything the interlude listed belongs here. Much of living well with others consists of adjusting the gate well.

The second is the aperture, which I now place at the center. A center, as Chapter 3 proposed, has no parts to rearrange. **The aperture is the openness of the center**, and openness is its only variable. It cannot be tuned like an instrument; it can be opened or obstructed.

The aperture faces Reality and truth. Its openness shows as curiosity, as willingness to see what is so, and as readiness to be corrected. **[RULING R3: aperture placed at the center, facing truth; the interlude's list now belongs to the body's gate. If you revise the interlude's sentence, reword this section's first paragraph.]**

The two openings can come apart. I can narrow the gate while keeping the aperture open: end a heated conversation, ask for a day, and still want to know what happened and whether I was wrong. I can also hold the gate wide while the aperture is shut: listen for an hour, nod throughout, and let nothing change my mind.

Knowing you are loved does not automatically mean knowing you deserve to be protected. I have called these two different knowings. The first belongs to the openness of the center. The second belongs to the work of the boundary, which includes knowing that refusal is an option. “Adjustable openness” stays true of both, in different senses. The gate adjusts by degree and kind: more or less, faster or slower, this and not that. The aperture adjusts only by being more or less open. A useful picture is a gate that is attended rather than shut: someone stands at it with eyes open, letting through what should pass and turning back what should not.

And sometimes the right use of the gate is to leave. No skill at tending a boundary replaces the ability to walk out of a room that is not safe.

### Four Conditions of Relation

The usual picture of how a relationship goes wrong is a single line. At one end people are too close; at the other, too distant. Health sits somewhere in between, a prudent compromise between intimacy and independence. On that picture, every gain in connection costs some selfhood, and every gain in selfhood costs some connection.

I think the picture is mistaken. Two qualities are in play, and they vary independently. *Connection* is how fully a person participates in a relationship or a wider whole. *Autonomy* is how fully that person remains whole within it: able to think their own thoughts, keep their own limits, and have a life beyond it. Two independent qualities give four conditions, not two ends of a line.

- **Integration**: connected and intact. A person belongs without disappearing.
- **Absorption**: connected, not intact. The bond is strong, and one person's wholeness has been given up to it, or taken by it. A partner, a leader, a group, or an institution acts as though it were the only real whole.
- **Isolation**: intact, not connected. Independence is kept by refusing relation.
- **Anomie**: neither. Belonging to nothing while lacking stable wholeness.

Health is integration: high connection and high autonomy together. It is a corner, not a midpoint. The framework's phrases for it are “participation without erasure” and “permeability without dissolution.”

The two-quality picture does two things the line cannot. It shows absorption and isolation as different losses rather than opposite excesses: one has lost autonomy, the other connection. And it can name the fourth condition at all. On a line, a person who belongs nowhere and holds no stable self has no position. *Anomie* is borrowed from sociology, where it named a breakdown of shared norms. **[SOURCE: verify Durkheim's use of anomie, e.g., *Suicide* (1897)]**

These are conditions, not types of people. One person can be integrated in a friendship, absorbed at work, and isolated from family in the same year. The names describe what is happening in a relation, and what is happening can change.

One question tests for the corner. Think of the people who have loved you well, which is not the same as the people who wanted you most. Did you become more yourself around them, or less? Closeness that makes someone less themselves as the price of staying close is absorption, whatever it calls itself.

### Stronger Wholes, Deeper Connection

If health is a corner, why does deep connection so often feel like a risk? Because for many people, at many times, closeness really has cost them themselves. The four conditions do not deny that. They locate the cost.

The framework offers a hypothesis: **the stronger a whole's internal integration, the more connection it can sustain without losing itself.** The difficulty is not strong connection as such. It is connection that exceeds a person's present capacity to remain whole.

If right, the hypothesis explains a familiar pattern. The same closeness that steadies one person can overwhelm another, and the same person can be overwhelmed at one time and steadied at another. It also changes the practical aim. The line says to keep connection moderate. The hypothesis says the aim remains the corner, and the way toward it may need pacing while a person's own wholeness grows.

This is a hypothesis, not a finding, and it can be checked. Family-systems research has long studied what it calls differentiation of self: roughly, the capacity to stay emotionally connected to others while keeping one's own thinking and sense of self. Questionnaires have been developed to measure it, and at least one distinguishes emotional cutoff from fusion with others, which resemble isolation and absorption. **[SOURCE: verify Bowen, *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice* (1978); Skowron and Friedlander, Differentiation of Self Inventory (1998), including its subscale names]** Whether that research bears the hypothesis out is a question for the evidence. If greater internal integration turned out not to predict a greater capacity for closeness without loss of self, the hypothesis would need to be revised.

### Belonging With a Life of One's Own

Boundaries do more than protect. They make independent judgment possible inside a relationship.

Where disagreement counts as betrayal, a relationship is moving away from integration and toward absorption. A family may demand loyalty at the expense of honesty. A group may treat a question as disloyalty. In such places belonging is bought with judgment, and the price tends to rise.

A boundary lets me hear another person fully without automatically adopting their view. I can take in what they say, weigh it, and answer from where I stand. That is not distance. It is what allows two people to remain two, and so to be in relation at all.

Recall the musician from Chapter 1. A good ensemble player listens closely, adjusts to the others, and blends. The same player keeps their own ear; if everyone only followed, no one would notice when the group drifted out of tune. And, as Chapter 1 put it, the player has a life the ensemble does not encompass. None of this weakens the ensemble; it is part of what each member brings.

A relationship between two people works the same way. Each brings a perspective the other cannot occupy. What they share is real: a history, a home, a way of talking that belongs to them both. What each of them is remains their own.

Asking someone what they would like treats them as the judge of their own life. Telling them “I know what you need” sets my view above theirs. There are times when one person must decide for another, as with a small child or in an emergency. Outside those times, asking is how one whole respects another.

## Chapter 5: When a Part Feels Like the Whole

This book began with a surge in my chest. I want to return to it now, with the distinctions of the first four chapters in hand.

### What the Surge Shows

In the prologue I described what happens when I think about my trauma. A surge begins in the center of my chest, and I experience it as adrenaline spreading outward. It fills my body and takes over my attention. In that moment, the reaction can feel like the whole of me.

Three things in that description matter here.

First, the reaction does not wait for permission. It can begin before I have chosen to spend any time with the thought. Many people describe reactions to past harm that start before any deliberate thought. **[SOURCE: research on trauma reactions triggered by cues before deliberate thought; candidates: Ehlers and Clark (2000); Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph (1996); verify]** That timing matters morally. What arrives before I choose is not something I chose.

Second, knowing is not the same as being ready. I know the difference between a memory and what is happening now, and knowing that difference does not always stop the feeling. A person can sincerely say that a bridge is safe and still freeze before stepping onto it. The sentence and the reaction disagree, and the disagreement is not a failure of intelligence.

Third, the question the surge raises is not how large it is. It is large: it reaches my attention and my whole body. The question is what it is allowed to stand for.

### Affecting the Whole Without Defining It

A part can affect an entire whole. A sprained ankle is one joint, and it changes how the whole body walks. Grief for one person can colour every room of a life. If “part” meant something small and contained, my trauma would not be a part of me. It is neither small nor contained.

Affecting the whole is still different from being the whole. Think again of the ensemble from Chapter 1.

For a few bars, one instrument can drown out all the others. While it does, it is all anyone hears. It is still one instrument. The piece holds more than those bars, and the other players have not stopped playing because no one can hear them.

That is the sense in which the prologue's sentence helps me: *My trauma is part of me. I am the whole.* It does not shrink the trauma. It declines to let the trauma speak for everything. Put as a principle: **the whole may contain a part without allowing that part to define the whole.**

Being whole, in this book's sense, never meant being calm, unhurt, or in control. Chapter 1 said that a whole can be unfinished, injured, or in conflict. The surge belongs to my life. So do the other aspects the prologue named, which do not cease to exist when I have trouble feeling them.

### Roles That Take Over

The prologue widened the question beyond trauma, to roles, to conflicts, and to the judgments of institutions.

Roles are real parts of a life. Parent, nurse, son, caregiver, the reliable one at work: each organizes days and carries real obligations. The trouble begins when one role becomes the whole answer to the question of who someone is.

The framework proposes a path by which this happens. A description is first entertained: “I'm the one who holds everything together.” Then it is repeated. Then it becomes habitual and runs without attention. Finally it fuses with identity: “This is simply who I am.”

At that last step, a challenge to the description no longer feels like a challenge to a description. It feels like a threat to the self. I offer this as a proposal about a common pattern, not as a clinical account. Chapter 6 takes up how repetition shapes later conditions.

Consider someone who learned early that safety depended on keeping everyone calm, and who is still keeping everyone calm decades later, in rooms where nothing depends on it. Habit can keep an old world going long after that world has ended. The role began as a way of surviving, and over time it has been taken for the person. The strategy deserves respect; it did its work. It is still not the whole person.

Chapter 3 kept three things apart: the *image*, a picture of me that is assembled and often wrong; the *self*, the whole of my life, real and changing; and the *center*, the point of view that whole is lived from. The distinction helps here. A role can harden into an image. The self is always larger than any role. The center was never a role at all.

### Two Directions of Failure

The surge and the role share the shape Chapter 2 called **a part standing for the whole**. One part, whether a feeling, a role, a conflict, an image, or a group, is treated as though it were the whole. Wholeness does not require any part to declare itself the only real part.

Chapter 2 also named the mirror failure: **a whole reduced to a part**, a person treated, or treating themselves, as only a function, a piece, or a role. An institution can see a diagnosis and miss the patient, see output and miss the worker, see a grade and miss the student. A person can come to see themselves the same way, as useful or useless and nothing else.

The two failures often travel together. When one part claims to be the whole, the rest is pushed out of view. When the surge stands for everything, my curiosity and affection are still present, but they are treated as absent. When one person or faction claims to be the whole of a group, the others are pressed to forget that they are whole themselves. Later chapters return to groups.

These names describe what happens, not what kind of person someone is. Anyone can fall into either direction, and many of us have been on both sides of the second: treated as a function, and treating others as one.

### Whole-Body Attention

What helps me is a thought joined to a shift in attention. I remind myself that the trauma is part of me and that I am the whole, and I try to feel my whole body, not only the center of my chest.

The practice does not ask me to deny the surge. It reminds me that there is more of me here to notice than the place where the surge first drew my attention. My attention has somewhere else to go, and what hurts can stay acknowledged while it goes there.

I offer this as testimony, not as a recommendation. Three cautions belong beside it.

First, it does not suit everyone. For some people, turning attention toward the body increases distress rather than easing it, and teachers who work with people affected by trauma have developed more careful approaches for that reason. **[SOURCE: verify Treleaven, *Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness* (2018); on meditation-related difficulties, e.g., Lindahl et al. (2017)]** My experience tells you about me, not about you.

Second, this book is not treatment. People living with trauma deserve qualified support, and nothing here substitutes for it.

Third, relief is not proof. The practice helps me, and that is part of why I want to understand it. Its helping does not establish that the framework's account of a center, a field, and a boundary is true. An image can steady a person without being a correct description of how things are.

### Acknowledging Without Developing

The practice rests on a distinction worth making explicit: acknowledging a thought is different from developing it.

When a memory or a possibility arises, attention can give it more detail and more life by rehearsing it, extending it, and building on it. Or attention can recognize it and decline to build. The truthful form of declining fits in two short sentences: *This possibility appeared. I have chosen not to develop it.* The same form fits a memory.

This is not pretending. Pretending says, “That never happened.” It is false, and falsehoods about one's own mind tend to need taking back. Acknowledging without developing says, “It happened, and I am not following it further now.” That stays true however closely I look later.

A thought has layers that need not open together: it occurred; it could mean something; it could be explored; it could be told to someone; it could be acted on. I can acknowledge the first layer while declining the rest.

Declining now is not refusing ever to look. There are times when looking closely, often with support, is what a person chooses. And declining is not always available.

One more thing must be said plainly: what arises is not a choice. Thoughts and memories return uninvited. Their return is something a mind does, not something its owner decided. Moral meaning begins with endorsement, deliberate cultivation, communication, or action, not with the arrival of a thought. No account of wholeness worth keeping should turn suffering into an accusation.

### What Wholeness Does Not Require

The prologue said that sometimes it feels as though I can turn down a valve, and that doing so is challenging. Both halves matter. The influence is real, and it is partial.

The interlude said that I can struggle with this and remain whole, and that a surge of feeling does not make me responsible for having failed to control every part of myself. I want to go further: **control is not the test of wholeness.** I am whole whether the surge passes quickly or slowly, whether the practice helps or not. Wholeness does not require the pain to be gone, or small, or understood. It names the life in which these things happen, not a mastery over them.

The framework describes mental health as range of motion rather than a fixed state: the freedom to move inward to rest and reflect, and outward to act and meet. On that picture, what a surge takes while it lasts is range. Range is worth valuing and worth regaining, but it is not a test either. A narrowed range is a condition a life is in, not a verdict on the one living it.

Help can come from more than one direction: through the body and its conditions, through other people, through meaning and attention. I do not think any one direction is the whole answer, because no one part is the whole of a person. Needing support is not a sign that a whole has failed. Every whole is also a part, and parts of greater wholes depend on one another.

## Chapter 6: The Flow and the Form

Chapter 5 rested on a small freedom: attention can move. When one part of a life fills the foreground, attention can widen to include more of the whole without denying the part.

Attention does not move across a blank surface, though. It moves through a body with a history, through habits, and through circumstances already in place. Where it moves again and again, it also changes the ground it moves through. This chapter is about that two-way relation: activity follows the conditions it finds, and repeated activity helps shape the conditions it will find later.

The interlude closed with a sentence I want to test: “I create enough structure to let myself flow, and enough openness to let myself learn.” What kind of structure lets anything flow?

### Structure to Flow Within

The interlude's phrase, *structure to flow within*, came from hours of editing connected points in virtual reality. Afterward the sense of the connected grid stayed with me, the way a boat's motion stays after a day on the water. Imagining the inside of my body as that kind of grid felt calming. It left me with an image of many parts remaining connected while they moved. **[AUTHOR: add more of the VR experience here if you want it; the draft adds nothing beyond the interlude.]**

We usually speak of structure and flow as two things. A riverbed is structure. A river is flow. One stays and the other passes.

Look longer and they are harder to separate. The bed is the shape that moving water has cut, and the river is water moving within that shape. The bed does not oppose the movement. It lets the movement go somewhere. Water needs a riverbed.

Some forms are made entirely of flow. A whirlpool behind a rock keeps its shape for as long as the current holds, though none of its water stays. A flame keeps its shape while its fuel and air are continually replaced.

The framework proposes that a life is a form of this kind: less like a rock than like a standing wave in a river, where the shape holds and the water keeps passing through. On this proposal, a body is not a thing that has an interface; the body is the interfacing, continuing. A mind is not a container in which relating happens; it is the relating, sustained.

The continuity of a life, likewise, is less like a rod running through time than like a rhythm kept. A rhythm is not a sound that never stops. It is a sound that reliably returns.

In each case, structure is a process seen while it holds its shape. That matters for change. If a life were a fixed object, we would have to ask how anything could alter it. If it is a form held by ongoing activity, then the activity that holds it can also, gradually, reshape it.

### Water and Rock

Water runs where the ground lets it run. The land decides most of its course. Given time, running water also changes the land. It deepens its channel and wears at its banks. A river follows its bed, and the bed is cut by the river.

That is the image. The proposal I draw from it has two parts.

First, mental activity follows existing conditions. A reaction takes the route that is already open. In the prologue, the surge in my chest could begin before I had chosen to spend any time with the thought. Two people who know each other well can fall into the same argument before either has decided anything.

Second, repeated participation can help shape later conditions. What I attend to again and again becomes easier to attend to. A response I repeat becomes a more likely response. The channel deepens. Something once done with effort can become something done without deciding.

The boat comparison is an everyday instance: a motion felt for hours can go on being felt after the water is gone. A pattern repeated long enough can outlast its conditions. I offer that as an observation about experience, not an account of how balance works.

The word *help* matters. Repetition is one condition among many. Water does not choose the slope or the hardness of the rock, and I did not choose most of the conditions in which my responses formed.

In a meditation I wrote for times of stress, two lines read: “When the banks were carved by others, I followed. When I carve my own banks, I arrive.” Those lines state a hope. Nobody carves all of their own banks, and the carving is slow. **[AUTHOR: confirm these lines from your meditation "Reclaim" (docs/reclaim_stress_meditation.html).]**

What people take to be possible changes what they attempt. What they attempt changes what they practice, and practice changes capacity. But some ground does not give way. Belief opens a path. Reality decides whether the path can be walked.

This is a proposal about practice, not a finding about brains or bodies. How repetition changes a living body is a question for the sciences of learning and habit, with their own evidence and mechanisms. The river may resemble what they find, or it may not.

### Firmness and Yielding

Water teaches a second lesson, about range. In the interlude I borrowed Bruce Lee's image of water for it: something that can give way around any obstacle and can also strike with force. **[SOURCE: likely the 1971 *Longstreet* episode "The Way of the Intercepting Fist"; verify wording]**

Martial artists know the same range as a distinction between styles: hard styles emphasize structure and direct force, and soft styles emphasize flow and redirection.

I trained for years with a teacher, Varpet Ed Saki, and later taught alongside him (*Varpet* is Armenian for master). **[AUTHOR: confirm use of the Varpet material and permission to name him]** What I learned from him is that mastery is not choosing one style. Mastery is knowing when to use which. I watched him hold a student to an exacting standard and, moments later, kneel to encourage a discouraged child. He was reading what the moment required.

Each style fails on its own. Pure rigidity breaks; pure yielding dissipates. The rigid response holds its shape until something stronger arrives, and then it cracks. The endlessly yielding response keeps contact by giving up its own direction, until little is left to keep contact with. In the terms of Chapter 4, rigidity tends toward isolation, intact but unconnected, and yielding without limit tends toward absorption, connected but no longer intact.

So the answer is not moderation, any more than health in Chapter 4 was a midpoint. A person who is always somewhat firm and somewhat yielding has not solved the problem. They have blurred it. The skill is range: firmness fully available when firmness is needed, yielding fully available when yielding is needed, and the judgment to tell which.

The interlude said that a firm boundary can be expressed gently. Varpet showed me what that looks like. When a child crossed a line in class, his response was always the same: “You can't do that here.” He did not tell the child they were bad. The standard did not bend, and the child still belonged.

I keep the lesson in a short form: focus on the behaviour, preserve the center. Firmness and gentleness need not compete, because they can be aimed at different things at once. The firmness is for the conduct. The gentleness is for the person.

### Changing a Response

Knowing that both are needed does not tell me which one this moment needs. That takes judgment, and judgment is harder when a response has already worn its channel.

Many of us have a default. Some lead with structure, others with warmth. A default may have fit the conditions in which it formed, and it can outlast them. Yielding that once kept the peace can later leave harm unaddressed. Firmness that once protected can later keep out a correction I need to hear.

Changing a response begins with noticing that one is under way before I have chosen it. Then I can ask a few questions. What is this response protecting, and is it still protecting that? What would the other response cost, and who would bear the cost? Can I be firm about the conduct and gentle with the person?

No rule settles these in advance. The interlude said that my values provide continuity while I reconsider how to act. They do not dictate the response. They keep me recognizably myself while it changes. Because judgment can be wrong, these choices also stay open to correction, which Chapter 11 takes up.

A changed response also has to be practiced. One good choice does not cut a new channel. The new response has to be taken many times, often while the old one still arrives first.

### Practice and Habit

Attention is something practiced. That can sound like a demand for control, so I want to be careful about what practice does.

Chapter 4 distinguished two openings: the aperture, which is the openness of the center, and the body's gate, which regulates what crosses, how much, how fast, and in what form. Practice works on them differently. The gate can be trained. I can learn when I need space, how quickly to answer, and what to let in.

The aperture, as the framework understands it, is not a skill. A center has no parts to arrange, so there is nothing in it to improve. It can only be open or obstructed. What practice can do for the center is clear away what obstructs it: hurry, certainty, an image I am protecting. Most of what we call training attention trains the paths attention takes through the mind, and the body's readiness to follow. **[RULING R3: practice is placed at the gate and in the mind; the center is only opened or obstructed. The interlude places the aperture in the boundary.]**

What improves with practice, then, is the return. Attention drifts or gets captured, I notice, and I bring it back or widen it again. Distraction is not failure. It is the rep, and a session with fifty returns is fifty repetitions. The whole-body attention of the prologue is a return of this kind rather than a state to be held. **[AUTHOR: what you have found with repetition, e.g., whether returning to whole-body attention has become easier, and over what span. The draft supplies none of this.]**

Practice can quietly become a test, and then it works against the whole. Early in my training, Varpet said that martial arts is just a vessel for raising good humans. In the tribute I later wrote to him, I added: “The vessel holds the water. It shapes the water. But you don't drink the vessel.” A practice is a vessel too, and it is easy to start drinking it: to judge a life by the unbroken streak, the perfect session, the reaction that never comes.

Practice does not make anyone whole. It keeps a whole in better contact with itself. A missed day does not break the thread of a life. Continuity is a matter of returning, not of never stopping.

A habit is flow that has cut a channel: a response that runs without deliberation because it has run many times in similar conditions. Habits are not failures of attention. Much of what any of us does well, we do by habit, from tying a shoe to telling the truth without first calculating whether to. Ethics becomes structure through repeated action, and structure becomes a world in which others must live.

Research on habit supports a modest version of the image. Habits appear to form gradually through repetition in stable contexts, at rates that vary widely between people and behaviours, and once formed they can run even after the intention behind them has changed. **[SOURCE: Wood and Rünger, "Psychology of Habit," *Annual Review of Psychology* (2016); Lally et al., "How Are Habits Formed," *European Journal of Social Psychology* (2010); verify]** That supports the image's shape, not any account of what happens inside a body.

### Breath, Movement, and the Body's Reply

The body is not only where habits live. It also answers. A tight chest, a held breath, a clenched jaw: these are reports. When I notice them, what I do can change, and what I do can change what the body reports next. I call that loop bodily feedback, in the ordinary sense of the words.

### An Image and an Explanation

This chapter has leaned on images: the riverbed, the standing wave, water and rock, the vessel, the grid. I want to say plainly what they do and what they cannot do.

An image helps when it shows me a relation I can check in my own life. The river says that activity follows its conditions and that repeated activity reshapes them. I can test that. Is a response easier the tenth time than the first, and does a return come more readily after months of practice?

Reasoned from as though it were the mechanism, the image would mislead. # Part III: Wholes Within Reality

## Chapter 7: Body and Earth

A river can hold its course for a very long time. It does not make its own rain.

Every structure that flows depends on conditions it did not make. The second half of the opening statement turns toward those conditions. It begins:

> I am part.  
> One part of Earth.

Chapter 1 described this pairing as my embodied life within the physical world that sustains it. Of the three pairings, it is the easiest to check. The next breath will do.

This chapter follows that pairing through exchange, supply, and limits, then asks what kind of whole Earth is.

### What Crosses the Edge

Take a breath. The air you just drew in was outside you a moment ago. Some of it stays with you for a while. The rest goes back out, changed. On a cold morning the change is visible: breath leaves as a small cloud, carrying water out of the body.

Water moves more slowly. Someone who drinks a few glasses a day takes in, over a year, far more than their own weight, and they do not grow heavier by that amount. Most of what comes in goes out again, as sweat, breath, and urine.

Food moves more slowly still: some of it becomes part of you for a time, and much of it passes through. Warmth moves both ways; a body makes heat of its own, and on a cold day it can lose heat faster than it makes it. Light arrives from farthest away. It lets you see, and it sets the rhythm of waking and sleeping.

In each case something crosses the edge of the body, the interface Chapter 4 described. Chapter 3 called that edge one surface with two names. From within, it is skin, lungs, the lining of the gut. From outside, it is the place where the world is already touching you.

In Chapter 2's terms, a body is one by distinction: set apart from what it is not. A body is set apart without being sealed off. Distinction is not a seal.

### Borrowed Material

If so much passes through, what makes this body yours?

Not the material. Much of a body's material is exchanged over the years, at rates that differ greatly from one tissue to another, and some may stay for life. **[SOURCE: rates of material and cell turnover in the human body, and what lasts for life; candidate: Spalding et al., “Retrospective birth dating of cells in humans,” Cell, 2005; verify.]** Material arrives from the world, stays for a while, and returns.

What persists is the organization: the way this body takes material in, arranges it, repairs itself with it, and lets it go. With the organization runs the thread of a life, the continuity that keeps it one through change. Chapter 6 described a whirlpool that keeps its shape while water passes through it. A body is far more than a whirlpool, but it shares that feature. Its form is its own. Its water is not.

The word I use for this is *borrowed*. The material of my body is borrowed from Earth. Some of it returns with every breath, and the rest returns in time.

This gives a first, exact sense to “one part of Earth.” Chapter 2 separated several ways of belonging. My body is not a member of Earth the way a person is a member of a club; Earth keeps no list. It is composed of Earth's material, but composition alone would make me part of Earth the way a stone is. The sense that matters most is participation: my body takes part in Earth's exchanges of air, water, and food while keeping an organization of its own, much as the musician in Chapter 1 takes part in an ensemble while having a life the ensemble does not encompass. I call this **material participation**.

### Supply and Limits

The exchanges above have a structure, and the rest of the argument rests on it.

**A whole's organization is its own. Its supplies are not.**

Limits are real on both sides. A body's limits concern what it needs, how often, and how much it can take in or bear. A supply's limits concern what is there and how quickly it returns. Set side by side, the five ordinary supplies look like this:

| Supply | What the body does with it | Where it comes from | Limits on each side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air | Breathes, faster in effort, slower at rest | The air around us, shared with everything that breathes | Needed every few seconds; a closed, crowded room grows stale |
| Water | Feels thirst, drinks, sweats, lets water go | Rain, rivers, wells, and pipes that others built | Needed daily, but not a week's worth at once; a well gives what the ground holds |
| Food | Feels hunger, eats, digests, stores a little | Plants grown from soil, rain, and light, and animals that ate plants | Hunger can wait longer, but not forever; a field yields what soil, rain, and season allow |
| Warmth | Makes heat, shivers, sweats, seeks shelter | Food, sun, fire; kept by clothing and a roof | Too little harms, and so does too much; the sun's warmth comes and goes with the seasons |
| Light | Sees by it; wakes and sleeps by its rhythm | The sun, and lamps that others built | Darkness hides, and glare dazzles; daylight keeps its own hours |

Each entry is an ordinary observation. The limits on the two sides are not a footnote to the body's organization. They are much of what that organization is for. If supplies were unlimited and arrived exactly when needed, and if a body could take in anything in any amount, there would be nothing to regulate. Thirst, hunger, shivering, sweating, and sleep at night are a body's ways of meeting supplies it does not control with limits it cannot remove. The body's gate, Chapter 4's name for the boundary's regulation of what enters and leaves, exists because limits are real on both sides.

That is the place supply and limits hold in this argument. What a whole does is its own. What it does it with comes from beyond it. What it can do is set by both.

### Organized and Dependent at Once

How can a whole have its own organization while depending on conditions beyond itself? The two descriptions can sound like rivals. If I am one whole body, should I not be self-contained? If I am one part of Earth, is my body really mine?

### From Dependence to Care

Dependence invites a quick inference: I depend on Earth, so I owe Earth care. The inference has real pull, but dependence alone cannot carry it.

## Chapter 8: Mind and the Greater Whole

Chapter 7 followed the body's exchanges with Earth: air, water, food, warmth, and light crossing its boundary. The mind exchanges something else. It takes in meaning and gives meaning back: a word, a look, a question, a promise.

In Chapter 1, I said that conversation makes this familiar. We share an exchange while each having a particular experience of it. What is shared when two people talk or work together? What stays private? And when many people share a great deal, as a family or a nation does, is there ever a further experiencer, a mind belonging to the group?

### One Exchange, Two Experiences

Imagine two people carrying a heavy table through a narrow doorway. They share a task, a timing, and a running commentary: a little to your left, wait, now lift. The table moves because both of them move it.

Yet each feels the weight in their own arms. One is watching the door frame near her knuckles. The other is thinking about his back. The task is shared. The strain is not.

Conversation has the same shape. When a friend tells me her news, the words pass between us and we both attend to them. My understanding of what she says can be good or poor, and she can correct it. What I cannot do is have her experience of telling it. I have my own experience of hearing it.

You can try a small version now. Read the word *harbour* and notice what arrives with it: boats, perhaps, or a smell of salt, a particular shoreline, or very little.

I wrote the word with my associations. You received it with yours. The word and its meaning passed between us. Our experiences of it did not.

Philosophers call one side of this the problem of other minds. I never observe another person's experience directly, so I cannot prove from outside that anyone else has experiences at all. Few people live as though that question were open. Whatever grounds our confidence that others are someone, it is not access.

### One Field, Many Local Organizations

Chapter 1 stated a proposal: inner and outer relationships belong to a common field, organized differently within and beyond a particular person.

In this book's terms, my mind is the field that arises within the inside my body gives my life. In it, the many aspects of my life relate: a sensation to a memory, a memory to a concern, a concern to a possible action. **The proposal is that these relationships do not stop at the edge of the person.** They continue into relationships with other people, with words and practices, and with the things we handle together. What I call my mind is a local organization of that wider field: organized around one center, within one body, carrying one history.

A language is the clearest example I know. English is real and shared, but it is not inside any one speaker. Each speaker carries a local organization of it: a vocabulary, an accent, a set of associations. No speaker contains the language, and the language has no speaker at its middle.

Yet when two speakers talk, the language is what lets meaning move between them. The proposal says that meaning in general works this way, and so do attention and concern.

Two cautions apply. First, *one field* does not mean one mind. A mind is organized around a center; the wider field, as far as I can tell, has no single center.

Second, *field* still names an organization of relationships, as it did in Chapter 1. Nothing here suggests that thoughts pass between heads without bodies, voices, or signs. Every relationship that reaches another person travels through the shared world: a face, a sound, a mark on a page, a hand on a shoulder. Connection between persons stays mediated.

### What the Proposal Explains

A proposal like this is worth keeping only if it does work that ordinary words were not already doing. Calling everything a field would explain nothing. I think this one does three things.

It explains shared meaning without shared experience. When you read *harbour*, the meaning reached you through relationships we both take part in: the word, its uses, the things it names. Your experience of it was organized around your own center, among your own memories. So two people can mean the same thing while experiencing it differently.

This holds together what two familiar pictures pry apart. One says we never truly share anything, since each of us is sealed in private experience. The other says that real sharing would require merging. On the proposal, we share relationships, and we do not share centers.

Philosophers have argued on separate grounds that what our words mean depends partly on our community and on the world we talk about, and that the mind reaches into the tools and practices it relies on **[SOURCE: verify Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975); Burge, "Individualism and the Mental" (1979); Clark and Chalmers, "The Extended Mind," *Analysis* (1998)]**. The proposal gives that work a picture.

It explains degrees of attunement. Strangers who share a language can exchange directions. Colleagues share terms and habits. Old friends can read a pause. On the proposal, these are larger and smaller regions of shared organization, and they grow with shared history and practice.

The same picture shows a common way of falling out of tune: answering my model of a person instead of the person. Long familiarity can make the model detailed enough to reply before the other has finished speaking, and then two models are talking. The repair is to enlarge the overlap again, starting with a question.

It explains privacy as position. If the field is common, why is experience private? Because each local organization has its own center, and no one else can stand there. **Privacy is not a wall around a mind. It is the fact that no one else can stand where you stand.**

A wall might be breached by enough openness or a clever enough device. A position cannot be handed over, because a point of view is not a content that could be copied. It is the place from which contents are had. This is why privacy and closeness are not rivals.

What would count against the proposal? If everything it explains turned out to be explained as well by sealed minds trading signals, the field would be a new name for an old picture. If two people were ever shown to have one experience from one shared point of view, rather than each having their own experience of shared input, privacy as position would fail. And if some group were shown to be a single experiencer, the field would have centers beyond persons, and the picture would need revising. Chapter 12 asks what evidence could bear on the last two.

### Communicating Is Not Sharing an Experience

Much longing in human relationships is a longing to close the gap entirely: to be known without translation, to feel what another feels exactly as they feel it. The proposal suggests why that wish cannot be granted, and why it does not need to be.

When I empathize with a friend, I have my own experience of her experience. I may feel sorrow because she does. My sorrow can be real, and close in shape to hers, and still be mine. Empathy is a meeting in the field, not a transfer of a center.

Checking understanding shows the same structure. To learn whether I have understood you, I cannot look inside your experience and compare. I can say back what I heard and ask. Understanding is confirmed by the other person's word, not by access.

This does not make closeness smaller. Intimacy is not the removal of the in-between. It is the in-between working so well that it becomes transparent.

A long, honest conversation can feel like no distance at all, yet count what is in play: two bodies, two voices, thousands of words, two histories behind every sentence. The in-between was never busier. It had become clear enough that we stopped noticing it.

We share a world and a field, not a point of view. The world we can point to together, where a claim can be checked by both of us, lives in that overlap. This does not mean that the physical world exists only where people meet; the moon does not wait for us to look at it together. What depends on our meeting is the shared world of meaning: a language, a body of evidence, an agreement, a home.

Picture two people who share a life as two overlapping circles. The overlap belongs fully to each circle and to neither alone; if either withdraws, it disappears. However far the circles overlap, there are still two centers, unless the circles are made to coincide, and then one person has disappeared into the other. Two whole people can share a home, a history, and children while each remains the one whose life it is. Their meeting does not, by itself, make a third experiencer.

### Groups, Systems, and Single Experiencers

Not every collection of people is one in the same way. Three kinds of organization need to be kept apart.

A single experiencer is what Chapter 3 described: one center, a boundary that gives an inside, an interior field, and continuity through time. Its many parts and relations answer to one point of view.

A connected group is several experiencers whose fields overlap around something they share: a purpose, a practice, a place, a story. A family, a choir, a team, a nation. Its members can speak, refuse, leave, and return, and its shared purposes can be argued over, amended, and dropped. Margaret Gilbert has argued that even two people walking together form what she calls a plural subject: having jointly committed to the walk, each owes the other something, which is why one may object if the other strides off without a word **[SOURCE: verify Gilbert, "Walking Together: A Paradigmatic Social Phenomenon" (1990)]**.

A coordinated system is a set of parts whose activities are fitted together by signals, rules, schedules, or a controlling hub. Signals along a railway line are set so that trains keep their distance. A thermostat and a furnace keep a room warm. Coordination can happen without anyone understanding the whole, and without anyone at all.

So the three differ in two ways: how many centers they have, and how their parts are joined. A single experiencer has one center, and its parts answer to it. A connected group has several centers joined by shared meaning. A coordinated system may have no center at all, and its parts are joined by signals and rules.

One collection can be more than one of these. A hospital is a connected group of people and a coordinated system of schedules and protocols.

Can a group also be a single experiencer? Eric Schwitzgebel has argued that if materialism is true, the United States is probably conscious, since a nation seems to meet the standards that materialist theories use to credit consciousness to individuals **[SOURCE: verify Schwitzgebel, "If Materialism Is True, the United States Is Probably Conscious," *Philosophical Studies* (2015)]**. Christian List and Philip Pettit have argued that some groups, such as a company with fixed procedures for deciding, are agents in their own right, holding judgments that can come apart from what most members believe **[SOURCE: verify List and Pettit, *Group Agency* (2011)]**.

Agency, though, is not experience. A group can decide without there being anything it is like to be the group, and a system can behave as if it had one aim without there being anyone whose aim it is. Coordination alone does not establish consciousness.

Chapter 2 offered a test, which I hold as a test and not as a verdict. **A single experiencer has one indivisible center. A group has a center-shaped region occupied by several.**

On the proposal examined in Chapter 3, a point of view cannot be divided. Half a point of view would not be a smaller point of view; it would be two. A group's center is different. Its purposes can be divided and renegotiated, factions can split away, members can leave and others join, and the group carries on. If that is what a group's center is, then it is a region held by several centers.

A leader does not change this. A group organized around one person has that person's point of view at its middle, and the point of view remains the leader's. A center with spokes is a hub, not a single experiencer.

The test has limits. It looks at structure, which can be observed, rather than at experience, which cannot be observed directly in anyone. It could misjudge a group whose center is indivisible in a way we have not learned to recognize. **[RULING R2: whether any group is a centered whole stays open; the test is offered as a test, not a verdict.]**

Consider the case closest to home: two people who share a life. ### Groups and the Four Conditions

Whatever their final status, groups are real and consequential. Languages, laws, sciences, traditions, and the networks of care that catch people when they fall are shared organizations of this kind. The question is not whether to belong but how.

Chapter 4 named four conditions of relation, and they describe membership as well as partnership. A member is integrated when connected and intact: taking part fully, and still able to disagree, refuse, and remain. A member is absorbed when belonging costs their wholeness: when the group requires them to surrender their judgment, or when a role grows until it is all the group sees of them.

A member is isolated when they keep their wholeness but share little, as in a membership that exists only on paper. And a person falls into anomie when no group offers either connection or a stable place to stand, so that they belong to nothing while lacking a settled self. Health is the corner where connection and autonomy are both high. A good group leaves its members more fully themselves.

The test bears on one danger in particular. When a group speaks of itself as a single experiencer, with one will that its members merely carry out, it claims a kind of oneness it may not have: integration around one center. ## Chapter 9: Soul and Reality

The opening statement ends with its widest claim: I am one part of Reality. Chapter 1 paired that line with the soul, my singular point of view, and worded the pairing with care: my point of view within Reality as a whole. The first two pairings concerned a body within Earth and minds meeting through a shared world. This one concerns existence itself.

It is also where the book's title is tested hardest. If everything is whole and part, what about Reality? It includes everything. What could it be part of?

### What “Within” Means

Chapter 1 said that my point of view is not inside every individual thing. It belongs within the all-inclusive reality in which things exist. Here is why I say *within*.

No one experiences from nowhere. Right now an experience is occurring: the one you are having as you read. Whatever Reality is, it includes whatever occurs. So your point of view is not outside Reality, looking in. It is one of the things that are.

*Within* does not mean membership in a finished collection, the way a marble is in a bag. Marbles in a bag are indifferent to one another, and the bag is one more object beside them. The things within Reality are related, each affecting others and affected by them, and Reality is not one more object beside them. Nor is it a finished list; what is real is still happening. *Within* means participation: being one of the things that are, related to the rest.

One formulation puts this in the first person:

> Experience is within existence. Existence contains many experiences. I am one experience of existence.

The formulation rules out two opposite errors. “I am everything” is a part standing for the whole. “I am separate from everything” is isolation raised into a claim about existence. Between them stands the plain claim: I am one experience within everything.

Two consequences follow. My view is partial: it meets Reality from one place, through one body, with one history. Partial is not false. A lens limits light, and that is how it forms an image.

And my view is not possession. Seeing Reality from here does not make it mine. Others see from elsewhere, and no one sees all of it.

### The Reach of the Title

Read as a universal claim, *everything is whole and part* seems to say that every existing thing is both. Is Reality also part of something?

**Reality is one by inclusion: nothing lies outside it.** A person is one in a different way, by integration: many parts answering to one center, within a boundary. Reality has no boundary of that kind, and the reason carries the argument.

A centered whole is set apart from what it does not include. Its boundary marks an inside and an outside. To be set apart in this way, there must be something beyond the boundary, and whatever has something beyond it is not all there is. It follows that no centered whole includes everything, and that what includes everything is not a centered whole. There is no greatest centered whole: not because we have failed to find it, but because the description asks for a boundary with nothing on its far side. **[RULING R1: Reality is one by inclusion, not a centered whole; Chapter 1's "all-inclusive whole" is read in the broad sense.]**

The same reasoning shows that Reality is not part of anything. To be part is to belong within a wider whole, and nothing is wider than what includes everything.

An image may help. Draw the book's symbol on a sheet of paper: a point inside a circle. The circle sets a whole apart, and beyond it there is more paper. Now imagine the paper without edges. Every drawing is on it.

The paper is not a larger circle drawn around the first, and it is not one more drawing. That is the relation I mean between a centered whole and Reality. Pressed too far, the image misleads, since real paper is one more thing.

So the title holds of every thing within Reality, taken one at a time. Each is whole: identifiable as one, and some things more strictly, as centered wholes. Each is part: it participates within Reality, and usually within nearer wholes and groups as well. Reality itself is not one more thing within Reality, any more than the edgeless paper is one of the drawings on it. The title speaks of things, and Reality is not among them as an item.

This is not a convenient exception. Chapter 1 already separated *every thing* from *everything*, and the title's grammar carries both. Nor does the answer change what *part* means.

A part, as Chapter 1 put it, belongs within a wider whole in some identifiable way. Reality is a whole in that chapter's broad sense, since we can identify it as one, by inclusion, and to be part of Reality is to belong within it by participation. I have not stretched *part* so that Reality could be part of itself, and I have not placed a larger whole outside Reality to make the title come out true.

Chapter 1 called Reality the limiting case. Now the limit can be stated: the all-inclusive is one, it includes every part, and it is part of nothing.

Nor does Reality's oneness give it a single point of view. A point of view is a view from somewhere. Reality is not somewhere; it is where every somewhere is. On this book's account, a point of view is the center of a centered whole. Reality has no center of its own, and it is not a centered whole.

Here Reality is not a whole of that kind, and no single scale of things is the fundamental one.

### Whole at One Scale, One at the Next

A whole does not stop being whole when it takes part in something larger. Chapter 1 showed this with a word, a whole made of letters and also part of a sentence.

Lives work the same way. From the inside, a person is a whole life, with a body, a mind, a history, and a point of view. From a household's side, the same person is one member: one place at the table, one voice in a decision. A household in turn is one participant in the life of a street or a town. A completed whole can function as one participant at a larger scale while its internal organization stays intact.

Appearing as one from above is not being only a point. Someone counted as one employee, one voter, or one case can come to see themselves that way, and that is a whole reduced to a part. It can also be self-administered, and then it becomes a convenience: I am only one person; the system did it. A part with no center of its own is a part with no responsibility, which is some of the position's appeal.

The opposite failure is a whole that forgets it is also part. It treats its own scale as the place where explanation stops and answers to nothing beyond itself: a person who cannot be corrected, an institution that recognizes no authority outside its walls. That is a part standing for the whole.

Recursion does not climb forever. Above a person there are households, towns, and nations, and Chapter 8 left open whether any of these is a centered whole. Above every whole there is more Reality, but not necessarily another centered whole.

**There is always more Reality. There is not always another centered whole.** The nesting of wholes does not end at a top. It opens into Reality, which is not one more level.

### Six Kinds of One

Chapter 2 introduced the kinds of oneness. This chapter needs all six.

Reality is one by *inclusion*: nothing lies outside it. A center is one by *indivisibility*: there is nothing in it to cut. A life is one by *continuity*: its moments stay connected. A body is one by *distinction*: it is set apart from what it is not. A mind is one by *coherence*: many relations belong to one interior. A centered whole is one by *integration*: many parts answer to one center.

Read the opening statement with these in hand. One whole body: one by distinction, with an organization of its own. One whole mind: one by coherence. One whole soul: one by indivisibility. I am whole: one by integration, and continuous through time. One part of Reality: within what is one by inclusion.

A single life carries five kinds of oneness at once and stands within a sixth.

Many confusions about oneness mistake one kind for another. Sameness is demanded as unity, as if a family were one only when everyone agreed. Belonging is enforced as merger, as if a couple were one only when neither had a life apart.

And inclusion is mistaken for integration. Because Reality includes everything, it is tempting to conclude that distinctions are unreal: the self an illusion, every difference a veil. That gets things backward. Reality's oneness excludes nothing, the distinctions included. If they vanished, meeting and relationship and agreement would vanish with them, since each requires distinct participants.

My body is made of matter that has belonged to other things and will again. My point of view is not shared in that way. Matter is shared; centers are not.

### Roles, Not Dimensions

Four of these kinds of oneness belong to the anatomy of a whole that Chapter 3 described, and they come in an order. A whole forms as a center gathers, a thread continues from it, a boundary closes, and an interior field fills what the boundary encloses: point, path, boundary, field.

The order has reasons. A center that does not hold dissolves, so continuity comes next. A thread that never closes encloses nothing, so the boundary follows. There is no inside until something has closed around one, so the field comes last.

The integrated whole is not a fifth piece added at the end. It is the four composed.

It is tempting to read the four as dimensions. A point has no extent, a path has length, a boundary is a surface, a field fills a volume. The analogy is suggestive, and it has limits.

The body is not literally a two-dimensional surface; skin has thickness, and there are organs inside it. The mind is not literally a three-dimensional volume; a memory has no width.

Geometry does not even fix the numbers. A circle drawn on a page has a one-dimensional circumference around a two-dimensional interior. A sphere has a two-dimensional surface around a three-dimensional volume. What stays constant is a relation: a boundary is thinner than what it bounds.

And integration adds no coordinate. A whole is not four-dimensional because it is whole. **[RULING R7: point, path, boundary, and field are presented as roles, not literal dimensions.]**

So the sequence names roles: where a whole is organized from, what keeps it one through time, what sets it apart and lets it meet the world, and where its relations are held. The same questions can be asked of a cell, a person, or a household, and the answers differ. And a whole that has all four roles within it can fill a single place in something larger.

### Infinitely Finite

In the interlude, I proposed that physical composition continues without a final layer, and that even apparent emptiness contains further physical reality. I called this *infinitely finite*: finite forms within an endless depth of relationships.

Start with its scope. The proposal concerns extended physical reality: whatever takes up space. It has two parts. Composition everywhere: no region is truly empty. Composition without end: no layer is final.

By further structure I mean more than the fact that any region can be divided in thought. I mean organization: smaller things with arrangements of their own. The proposal says there is always such structure, not what it is. It is a hypothesis, not a finding. **[RULING R10: "infinitely finite" is presented as a hypothesis about extended physical reality.]** **[AUTHOR: confirm you mean further organized structure, not mere divisibility; only the strong reading is a substantive claim.]**

Three reasons motivate it. The first two bear on its endlessness, the third on its reach.

The first is the record so far. Again and again, a layer once taken as basic has turned out to have parts. Atoms were named for being uncuttable, yet they contain a nucleus and electrons. Nuclei are made of protons and neutrons, and those of quarks bound by gluons **[SOURCE: standard history; e.g., Pais, *Inward Bound* (1986)]**.

The record is suggestive, not proof. A long run of discoveries does not guarantee the next one.

The second is conceptual. An extended thing fills a region, and a region has smaller regions within it: a left and a right, a near side and a far side. It is hard to make sense of something that fills space while having no parts at all. Some philosophers have defended that possibility **[SOURCE: verify, e.g., Markosian, "Simples," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* (1998)]**. So this is an argument, not a proof, and it reaches only things that are extended.

The third concerns apparent emptiness. Physics no longer treats empty space as simply nothing. In current theory even a vacuum is a state of fields, with measurable effects, and space itself can bend and carry waves **[SOURCE: the quantum vacuum and the Casimir effect (Casimir 1948; Lamoreaux 1997; Jaffe 2005 on its interpretation); gravitational waves (Abbott et al. 2016)]**. What looks empty may be empty only of what a given instrument detects, the way the air between my hand and this page looks like nothing to the eye. But *not nothing* is not the same as *made of parts*, and the proposal needs the second.

The question is old. Kant set the thesis that composite things are made of simple parts against the antithesis that nothing in the world is simple, and argued that reason, left to itself, can build a convincing case for each **[SOURCE: verify Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, A434/B462]**. Leibniz held that every portion of matter is divided without end, like a garden full of plants whose every branch is another garden, while the beings that perceive are simple **[SOURCE: verify Leibniz, *Monadology* §§65 to 67]**. More recently, Jonathan Schaffer has argued that nothing in our evidence requires a fundamental level **[SOURCE: verify Schaffer, "Is There a Fundamental Level?" *Noûs* (2003)]**.

### What Cuts Against It

No substructure has been found in electrons or quarks. Experiments have probed them at very small distances and found nothing inside, and the standard theory of particle physics treats them as elementary **[SOURCE: Particle Data Group, *Review of Particle Physics*, searches for quark and lepton compositeness]**. That is not proof that they have no parts. It is the current edge of the record, and the record was the first reason for the proposal.

At those depths, *part* may also be the wrong word. Quantum theory describes particles as excitations of fields, and the familiar picture of a thing made of smaller things may not carry over. If composition stops applying there, the proposal owes an account of what *further structure* means where parts in the ordinary sense do not.

Then there is the Planck length, a scale far below anything experiments reach, where many physicists expect current ideas of space and distance to break down. Whether it marks a smallest meaningful distance is unknown **[SOURCE: verify Hossenfelder, "Minimal Length Scale Scenarios for Quantum Gravity," *Living Reviews in Relativity* 16 (2013)]**. The interlude said that even this scale would not be the final layer. If distance itself gives out there, that claim needs a sense of structure that does not depend on distance. I do not yet have one to offer.

Last, evidence can work on the hypothesis in only one direction with any force. Finding further structure would support it. Failing to find structure never shows there is none, since a finer instrument might find it. Only a well-tested theory that required a final level would count strongly against it.

A claim that observation can support but hardly refute is closer to a stance than a finding, and I say so plainly. Chapter 12 asks what would move it either way.

### A Center with No Size

How does endless composition fit with the proposal of an indivisible point of view?

If extended things have parts at every depth, it might seem that the point of view must be one more part, the smallest, waiting at the bottom. It is not proposed as anything of the kind. A point of view has no size to divide. It is not a tiny object inside the body. It is the position from which a whole life is lived.

The hypothesis of endless composition concerns things with extent, and a center has none, so the center is neither a counterexample nor a final layer. Leibniz's picture had the same shape.

That leaves a real question open. How an unextended center relates to an endlessly composed body is not answered by saying that it does. Chapter 3 gave the book's present account, a center as the point a whole is organized around and lived from, and that account still has work to do.

Endless composition also does not mean that every part has a point of view. Whether a given part is a centered whole is the separate question of Chapter 3's criteria.

Apparent emptiness looks different from here as well. What my point of view registers as empty depends on what it can resolve. There is more Reality than any view takes in. That is not a flaw in the view. A view that took in everything would be a view from nowhere.

Finally, endless depth needs nothing outside Reality. The descent runs inward, within each thing, not outward toward a final container.

A string fixed at both ends can sound its lowest tone and, at the same time, higher tones that divide the same length into halves, thirds, and quarters, each adding still points along it. In the ideal case there is no last such tone, and the string never grows. That is an image, not evidence. It shows only that a finite form and an endless series of divisions are not at odds.

Reality includes all the depths because it includes everything. It does not need to be a larger whole to do so.

# Part IV: Possibility and Responsibility

## Chapter 10: What the Present Carries

You have never lived anywhere but now. You remember yesterday now. You plan tomorrow now. Even this sentence, begun a moment ago, is being finished in a present that keeps arriving.

Chapter 9 placed my point of view within Reality. A point of view does not stretch across time the way a road stretches across a landscape. It lives in a present. This chapter asks what that present carries: what it inherits from the past, what it meets from the future, and what happens within it.

The question matters for the chapters that remain. If the present were only a thin slice between two larger regions of time, what we do in it might seem small. If the present is where whatever becomes actual becomes actual, then it is where every action is taken and every responsibility begins.

### The Past Is Carried in the Present

Much of the past is not somewhere else. It is here, in the shape of things.

A scar carries an injury. A path worn across a lawn carries many earlier footsteps. A habit carries the times something was done before. A law carries decisions made by people who are no longer present, and a language carries the choices of speakers no one now living has met. A valley carries the work of water. A letter, a photograph, or a court record carries an account of what happened.

A body carries the past in the same way. Skills learned years ago are present in how the hands move, though most of the learning is forgotten.

Even perception arrives a little late. When thunder reaches me, the lightning that made it is already over. Sunlight left the sun minutes before it reaches my eyes **[SOURCE: light travel time from the Sun, about eight minutes; any standard astronomy reference]**. By the time anything reaches me through my senses, it has already happened. The framework puts this simply: the body is where the past arrives.

The prologue described a surge that can begin with a stray thought about my trauma. In this chapter's terms, the surge is the past arriving in the present. The events are over. What they left includes a way my body can respond, and that response can begin now, before I have chosen to spend any time with the thought. I know the difference between a memory and what is happening now. The surge shows that the past is not only remembered. It is also carried. **[AUTHOR: optional: a few sentences on how memory and the present feel different to you during the surge, and what, if anything, changes when you notice that the surge is now and its source is past.]**

### Four Lines on Time

I have come to state the framework's account of time in four lines:

> We use the past to predict the future.  
> The future supplies possibility.  
> The past supplies constraint.  
> The present is where possibility moves through inherited constraint and becomes actuality.

The first line describes something we do constantly. A forecast uses past weather to estimate tomorrow's. We expect a friend to be late because the friend has been late before. Every expectation draws on what has already happened, because the past is the only place the materials for an expectation can come from.

The second line says what the future is: not yet settled. It is a range of possibilities, and possibility is not one thing. Something may be possible without being likely. Something may be likely without being wanted. Something may be wanted without being available. Keeping those apart is much of what practical thinking does.

Some philosophers, following Aristotle's discussion of a sea battle that may or may not happen tomorrow, have held that statements about a genuinely open future are not yet true or false **[SOURCE: verify Aristotle, De Interpretatione 9]**. Others disagree. The framework leans toward the open future: possibilities are not yet truths, and truths are what actualization leaves behind. I offer this as a position, not a settled result.

The third line says what the past contributes. It does not contribute possibility. It contributes the conditions within which possibility can become something definite.

The fourth line joins them. Consider a conversation. What I could say next is open. What I can actually say is shaped by my language, my history with this person, and what was just said. In the present, one thing gets said, and it becomes part of what the conversation carries forward.

The idea that the present is where possibility becomes actual has distinguished company. Aristotle distinguished potentiality from actuality, and Alfred North Whitehead described each moment of experience as many inherited influences becoming one new occasion **[SOURCE: verify Aristotle, Metaphysics IX; Whitehead, Process and Reality, 1929]**. The framework's way of putting it is its own, but the territory is old.

### Constraint Gives Form

The word *constraint* can sound only negative. In this account it is formative.

A lens limits light, and that limit is how it forms an image. Grammar limits which strings of words count as sentences, and that limit lets a sentence mean something. The rules of a game make play possible. Chapter 6's riverbed shapes the water that flows within it. If nothing constrained what happened, nothing in particular could happen. Possibility becomes actuality by passing through constraint, not in spite of it.

The past has two faces. One is structure: the scar, the habit, the institution, the riverbank. Structure can change. A scar fades, a habit loosens, an institution is reformed, a bank erodes. The other face is the record: the fact that something happened at all. The record cannot un-happen. A house can be demolished; that it stood, and that people lived in it, remains true.

Inheritance carries the structure. Prediction reads the record. Honest prediction reads the record and never rewrites it.

That distinction will matter in Chapter 11. No one can make what happened unhappen. What a person can do is fight its acknowledgment: insist it did not occur, discredit those who remember it, or retell it so that the roles are reversed. Such efforts cannot change the past. They can only change what is allowed to be said about it.

### A Meeting with No Width

Where, then, is the present?

The framework proposes that the present is located at the center: **the center is where the future and the past meet, and that meeting is the present.** Chapter 3 proposed that the point of view is indivisible. On this account, the present is found at that same point.

A meeting has no width. The present is not a very short stretch of time, because any stretch, however short, can be divided into a part already past and a part still to come. Augustine noticed this and concluded that what is strictly present has no length **[SOURCE: verify Augustine, Confessions XI.15]**. The framework accepts the observation and reads it this way: the present has no length because it is not a stretch at all. It is the place where what is coming meets what has been.

Each center has its own present. The framework does not claim one universal now spread across everything. Physics, for its own reasons, also gives up a single now shared by all observers **[SOURCE: relativity of simultaneity; any standard text on special relativity]**.

Two further lines describe the movement through that meeting:

> The future converges. The past emerges.

Many things that could happen narrow, at the center, into the one thing that does. What has happened then stands behind, settled into structure and record. The two lines leave the present unspoken, since it is where they meet. When I want to name it, I add a third line between them: *The present turns.*

These lines are not about signals. Every signal comes from the past; the thunder and the sunlight were already history when they arrived. What converges from the future is possibility, and possibility does not travel to me through any channel. It meets me where I am.

### The Openness of the Present

This brings back the aperture.

In Chapter 4, I distinguished two openings. The body's gate regulates what crosses between me and the world: how much, how fast, and in what form. The aperture is the openness of the center itself. **[RULING R3: the aperture is the openness of the center; the interlude's sentence placing it in the boundary is left for the author.]**

Time gives the distinction a further sense. The past reaches my center through my body and my mind: perceptions, memories, habits, all already formed. The future reaches my center through no channel, because it is not yet anything that could travel. So, on this account, my relation to the future has one variable: how open I am to what has not yet happened. The framework proposes that the aperture cannot be tuned, only opened or obstructed.

Openness to the future includes openness to correction, because to be correctable is to let my account become something it has not yet been. A mind that defends a settled picture of itself against everything new has turned to face backward. The grudge rehearsed for years, the old argument relitigated, the doctrine protected against evidence: each treats a piece of the past as a destination rather than a supply.

This is not a rule against looking back. Memory, mourning, gratitude, and learning from history all read the past, and a life needs them. The difference lies in whether the past is passed through on the way to the present or held up as a wall against it.

A life, I think, needs all three: a future that is still open, a present that someone is living, and a past that is acknowledged as having happened.

### What Happens Now Shapes What Can Happen Next

Earlier events shape later possibilities. That is the second and third lines read together.

Chapter 5 described how one part of a life can take over attention. Chapter 6 described how flow follows existing conditions and how repeated flow can reshape them. The present is where both happen. What I do now becomes part of the structure that later presents inherit. A response repeated becomes a habit. A promise kept becomes a history of keeping. A word said becomes something the other person carries.

This gives practice its meaning in time. I cannot choose what I inherit. I can take part in what I leave. Each present adds to the record and contributes, sometimes only slightly, to the conditions in which later possibilities will meet constraint.

That is not a promise of control. Many conditions are not mine to change, and some changes take a long time. It is a description of where participation happens: always now, always within inherited conditions, and always with some consequence for what comes next.

### Beginningless Convergence

The account so far concerns any present. A further question is whether the process ever began.

The framework calls the forming of a present center convergence: possibilities gathering toward a point where something becomes actual. I propose that this convergence is best understood as an ongoing relation rather than an event at the start of time. I call this beginningless convergence. **[AUTHOR: confirm the intended reading: (a) no first moment is assumed, and convergence is an ongoing relation compatible with a finite cosmic past; or (b) the stronger claim that convergence has no first instance.]**

In its modest form, the proposal says that the account of how a present forms does not assume a first moment and does not need one. What holds a center together now is not an event at the beginning but relations at work now. The open horizon of possibility is not behind us, like a starting line we have left. It is ahead of every present center, the way a blank page does not merely come before a drawing but remains available for every drawing still to be made.

There is a precedent for separating these questions. Thomas Aquinas held, as a matter of faith, that the world had a beginning, while arguing that reason alone could not prove it, and that created things depend at every moment on what holds them in being **[SOURCE: verify Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I q.46 a.2 and I q.104 a.1]**. Where things came from and what sustains them now are different questions.

The proposal is not a claim about physical cosmology unless it is stated as one. Cosmology describes the observable universe as having expanded from a hot, dense state roughly fourteen billion years ago **[SOURCE: e.g., Planck Collaboration 2018 cosmological parameters]**. Whether that marks an absolute first moment of time is still discussed **[SOURCE: needed; a survey of cosmological models with and without a beginning]**. If time had a first moment, the stronger reading would face a direct challenge, while the modest reading would remain available.

The stronger reading also meets philosophical objections. The kalam argument holds that an infinite series of past events could never have been completed **[SOURCE: verify; e.g., Craig, The Kalam Cosmological Argument, 1979]**. Kant argued that reason produces apparently sound arguments both for and against a beginning of the world in time **[SOURCE: verify Kant, Critique of Pure Reason, first antinomy, A426/B454]**. A reader persuaded by these arguments has reason to prefer the modest reading. A reader who wants to know how everything started may find the modest reading unsatisfying, since it answers a different question: not what began things, but what holds them.

### Energy, Power, and Mass

The framework states the movement through the present a third way: **energy is what could happen; power is what is happening; mass is what has happened.**

These are words from physics, and in physics they mean something else. Energy is the capacity to do work, measured in joules. Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or converted, measured in watts; one watt is one joule per second. Mass measures a body's resistance to changes in its motion and the strength of its gravitational attraction **[SOURCE: standard definitions, and mass-energy equivalence below; e.g., the SI Brochure (BIPM) or an introductory physics text]**. None of these is a tense. A rolling ball has energy now. A stone has its mass today, as it did yesterday.

So the triple is not a physical claim. It is a philosophical reading of time's structure that borrows these words for what they suggest. Energy suggests capacity: what could be done. Power is the only one of the three defined as a rate, and a rate is something happening. Mass suggests what has settled into stable form. **[RULING R6: the triple is presented as a reading of time's structure, not as physics.]**

Physics does hold that mass and energy are equivalent, so that one can be converted into the other. That is a statement of equivalence, not of sequence, and this reading does not rest on it. The triple helps me hold the four lines in mind. It predicts no measurement, and no measurement could confirm it.

The present, then, carries the past as structure and record, meets the future as possibility, and is where one becomes the other. If that is right, what we do in the present matters, because the present is the only place anything is ever done. The next chapter asks how to live there as whole and part.

## Chapter 11: Living as Whole and Part

Chapter 10 ended where action begins. The present is where possibility becomes actual, and what becomes actual stays. Whatever I do enters the record and becomes part of what other people inherit.

This chapter asks how to act well as someone who is both whole and part. When I consider an action, I try to keep three things in view: the person affected, their relationships, and the consequences within the Reality we share. The first is a whole. The second is that whole's participation. The third is the wider setting in which every participant lives.

All three can be described without ethics, and a description is not a verdict. To reach a verdict I need values, and I want to state mine openly.

### The Premises, Stated Openly

Being part of Reality does not by itself establish a duty. A stone is part of Reality, and nothing follows from that alone about what anyone owes it. Interdependence tells me whom my actions reach and how. It does not tell me why that should matter.

So here are my premises. People's experience matters. Their agency matters. Their welfare matters. By experience I mean what it is like to be them. By agency I mean their capacity to judge and choose for themselves. By welfare I mean the conditions under which their lives can go well.

The premises do their work when they meet facts about connection. Imagine I play music loudly at two in the morning. The sound crosses into my neighbour's home: a fact about connection. It becomes a reason to turn the music down because my neighbour's rest is part of their experience, their say over their own night is part of their agency, and their sleep bears on their welfare. Without the facts, the premises have nothing to act on. Without the premises, the facts are only acoustics.

The framework makes a further proposal: that these values are not arbitrary, because they track the conditions under which wholes stay whole. A person whose experience is ignored, whose agency is overridden, or whose welfare is consumed is pushed toward the failures this book has described: absorbed into something else, or cut off from what sustains them. I find that proposal persuasive. My argument here does not depend on it. The values are premises, and I state them as premises. **[RULING R4: values stated as premises; derivation from the structure of wholeness appears only as a further proposal.]**

### Five Questions

I propose five questions for practical reasoning: questions of truth, faithfulness, good, right action, and integrity. They do not compute answers. They organize attention, so that the person affected, their relationships, and the consequences are less likely to be missed.

I take them in this order because it follows the anatomy this book has described: an open center, a continuing thread, a boundary where lives meet, a field of relationships, and the whole they compose. That correspondence helps me remember the questions. It does not prove they are the right ones.

*Truth: is this true, and would I let it be corrected?* Truth concerns more than whether each sentence is accurate. Suppose a colleague asks whether a project is on schedule, and I describe every task that is on schedule and say nothing about the one that is not. Each sentence is accurate; the impression is false. Compare someone who gives a stranger directions and gets one turn wrong. That is honest error. The first person spoke accurately and deceived; the second spoke inaccurately and did not. A lie is worse on both counts. Research on misleading with true statements suggests that people who discover it judge it much as they judge lying **[SOURCE: verify; e.g., Rogers et al., “Artful Paltering,” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2017]**.

*Faithfulness: does this keep faith with my commitments, and with what they were for?* Faithfulness is continuity in ethical form: the thread that connects my word today to my action tomorrow. If I promised to help a friend move on Saturday, a more pleasant invitation is not a reason to disappear. Faithfulness is not blind loyalty, though. Loyalty to a person can become betrayal of truth, and loyalty to an institution can become betrayal of its purpose. Sometimes keeping faith with a friendship means naming a problem in it.

*Good: does this let both of us remain whole?* Here goodness lives where lives meet. It is what happens between people without destroying either one. It fails in two directions: one person consumed for another's benefit, which is exploitation, or a person left alone where connection was owed, which is neglect. In Chapter 4's terms, the good keeps a relationship at integration rather than absorption or isolation. Costs are not the problem. A parent who loses sleep over a sick child pays a cost, and that is love. A cost becomes harm when it hollows someone out or only ever flows one way.

*Right action: given how this will land, is it the fitting thing to do?* Right action concerns the field of relationships an act enters: who is affected, what follows, and whether the effects land fairly. Good intentions are not enough. Imagine a manager who corrects an employee's mistake, accurately and with good intent, in front of the whole team. The correction is true and the intention good, and the act still lands as humiliation. The same words in private might have been right.

*Integrity: do my reasons, words, and actions hold together, and could the people affected genuinely agree?* The word comes from the Latin for whole and untouched. Within one person, integrity means not saying one thing and doing another, and not keeping one standard for others and a softer one for myself. Between people, integrity takes the form of agreement: a shared decision in which each person remains whole. Agreement is not compliance or silence. Imagine a family deciding where an aging parent will live, in which one sibling goes along only to keep the peace. The family has a decision. It does not yet have agreement, because that sibling did not feel free to refuse. **[RULING R12: the framework names the fifth question “agreement”; this draft uses integrity, with agreement as integrity between people.]**

### Openness Is Not Automatic Agreement

In the interlude, I called corrigibility the willingness to remain open to correction. Here I want to say what kind of openness I mean.

Corrigibility is openness to justified correction. The word *justified* carries weight. A correction is justified when evidence or good reasons support it, and openness to that differs from agreeing with whoever is speaking.

Automatic agreement can look like humility, but it is closed in its own way. A person who yields to every objection lets the loudest voice, the most powerful person, or the most recent speaker revise their account. That is yielding to pressure, not to reality. A friend who never pushes back on anything offers agreement without correction, which leaves me alone with my mistakes.

The framework states the difference in one sentence: **the truthful person yields to evidence and resists pressure; the liar yields to pressure and resists evidence.** When my account changes, I can ask what changed it. Was it something I learned, or something I feared?

Every expectation is a box I place on the world before the world arrives, built, as Chapter 10 noted, from the past. When something does not fit, there are two honest moves: revise the box, or look again more carefully. The dishonest move is to revise the witness, by denying the misfit or discrediting whoever noticed it. A misfit is how Reality gets a word in.

This also means that being wrong is not the same as being untruthful. It is okay to be wrong. I can hold a mistaken belief honestly if I hold it in a way that lets it be corrected. The posture I aim for is: this is what I currently see; show me where I am wrong.

### Why Being Wrong Stings

Corrigibility takes courage because correction costs something.

When I am shown to be wrong, several things can break: a belief I relied on, an image of myself as careful or kind or competent, a reputation among people whose opinion matters to me. A choice I made can turn out to have hurt someone. Each of these is made of parts: claims, memories, other people's impressions. Things made of parts can break, and their breaking hurts.

Chapter 3 proposed that the point of view itself is indivisible. If that proposal is right, a correction can break what I believed and what I pictured, but not the center from which I do the believing. I find this steadying. It does not make correction painless. It makes the pain survivable, because what breaks is not the one who feels it.

Courage, then, is staying present while those things take the hit: feeling the sting without fleeing into denial or attack, and letting what is true change what I do. Responsibility for harm belongs here. If I have hurt someone, the record cannot be undone; Chapter 10 called that the past's second face. What I can do is acknowledge the harm plainly, without “if” and without explaining it away; attend to its effects on the person I hurt; repair what can be repaired; and change the conduct that caused it. Acknowledgment without change is incomplete. Change without acknowledgment leaves the other person alone with what happened. Accountability does not require giving up my limits, either. I can own what I did without accepting that it is all I am. **[AUTHOR: a correction you accepted or an apology you made could go here, in your own words, if you want one.]**

### When the Accusation Is Unfair

Corrigibility does not mean accepting every accusation. The interlude said that I can challenge an accusation that is mistaken or unfair, and that a boundary supports both my willingness to learn and my ability to say no. The same sentence about evidence and pressure applies, from the other side.

An unfair accusation may arrive carried by pressure rather than evidence: volume, repetition, numbers, a threat to belonging, or the suggestion that only a guilty person would object. Pressure is not a reason. I can ask what the evidence is. I can state my own account plainly. I can separate what I did from what I am accused of, accept the part that is true, and decline the part that is not. When the stakes are high, I can ask for a fair third party and keep a record of what was said.

There is a harder case. Sometimes a person presents themselves as the voice of reality in order to turn my willingness to revise against me: “You always get this wrong. You can't trust your own memory.” The framework calls this hijacked corrigibility, and it describes much of what people mean by gaslighting. The defence is the same question as before: what is revising my account, evidence or pressure? Openness to correction is not owed to pressure dressed as evidence.

For both situations, being corrected and being accused, I hold one line: **stand firmly at your center; keep the connection open.** The first half lets me refuse what is false. ### Protecting an Image

In the interlude, I described the pattern of protecting an image at the expense of truth, and called it narcissistic defensiveness. Here I keep to behaviour, because the word also names a clinical diagnosis, narcissistic personality disorder, and this book does not diagnose anyone.

The pattern has a familiar shape. The psychologist Jennifer Freyd named its core DARVO: deny, attack, and reverse victim and offender **[SOURCE: Freyd 1997, Feminism & Psychology 7(1); verify]**. Imagine a colleague reminded, with the email on the screen, of a promise to send a report by Friday. “I never said Friday.” “You're always keeping score.” “Honestly, I'm the one who's been let down here.” The email has not changed. The conversation has moved away from it.

Anyone can do this. Milder versions are familiar: “I don't think I ever said that.” “You're being a little sensitive.” “It hurts that you would think that of me.” Because the moves are common, their presence alone proves little. A person falsely accused will deny the accusation, and may well be angry. Denial and anger cannot, by themselves, tell a protected image from an innocent person.

What distinguishes a protected image, I have proposed, is the profile of the behaviour over time: the defence recurs across very different situations; it fires even at low stakes; it is unmoved by the quality of the evidence; it goes after the person raising the point rather than the point; and it persists after the challenge has been withdrawn. These are proposed markers for research, not established findings.

There is also a test anyone can run on themselves at the edge of a belief they care about. Can I name the belief, explain why I hold it, and hear the strongest argument against it without going deaf? If I can, I hold the belief. If getting near it makes me foggy, defensive, or deaf, the belief is holding me. The short form: hold your limits; do not let them hold you.

The framework offers a proposal about why the pattern can become so fierce. When a person's sense of where they stand moves from their own point of view into an image of themselves, every correction of the image feels like an attack on the self. A point of view has no parts for a fact to break. An image is made of claims, and claims can be contradicted. This is a proposal about a pattern anyone can fall into. It is not a clinical account of narcissistic personality disorder, which would need its own research. **[RULING R5: behaviour, not labels; not a clinical account; the point of view remains present.]**

The proposal also keeps a limit the interlude set: it claims no change in the soul itself. On this account the center is denied, not destroyed: the person lives as if they were the image, while the point of view remains. The person is still present and still able to return. That shapes the right response: address the behaviour, protect the people it harms, and leave the way back open.

### When Claims Compete

The five questions do not always agree. Truth can pull one way and welfare another. Faithfulness to one person can pull against fairness to another. Then the questions organize the problem, and judgment has to do the rest.

Consider truth and welfare. Imagine that a friend whose marriage has just ended asks me whether I always thought it would fail. Suppose I did have doubts. Saying “Never” would be a lie. Listing every doubt I ever had, on the day the marriage ended, would serve my wish to be right more than my friend's need. The framework proposes a third path: say the true thing at the resolution the other person can use, and say more if they ask. “I had some worries along the way. I also saw how much you put into it.” Both sentences are true. If my friend wants more, I can give more without retracting anything.

That is the test. If I can add detail later without taking back what I said first, I told the truth at a lower resolution. If adding detail would force a retraction, I told a comfortable fiction. Withholding everything and dumping everything both fail: the first dressed as kindness, the second dressed as honesty.

There is a caution. “I was protecting them” is a familiar excuse for managing someone else's understanding. So I ask two further questions. Would I accept this handling if our positions were reversed? Am I preserving my friend's ability to judge, or replacing it with my own? As far as possible, the resolution belongs to the person receiving it.

Now consider loyalty and fairness. Suppose a close friend at work presents a teammate's idea as his own in a meeting. Faithfulness to my friend pulls one way; fairness to the teammate pulls the other. Faithfulness aimed at what matters most does not require helping my friend keep an unfair gain. It might mean speaking to him privately first, so that he can correct the record himself. If he will not, the teammate has a claim that friendship cannot cancel. The symmetry question helps here too: if I were the teammate, what would I want from someone standing where I stand?

Not every conflict involves a lie or a mistake. Two honest people can see a situation clearly and still want incompatible things when there is not enough to go around. Someone will lose, and no one deceived anyone. The five questions cannot remove that loss. They can help make the decision truthful, faithful, good to everyone it touches, attentive to consequences, and as genuinely agreed as the situation allows.

One rule governs all of this, and it applies to me first: if my analysis exempts me, it is not an analysis; it is a weapon. **[AUTHOR: confirm use of this line from your essay “What Makes Harm Last.”]**

### No Group Is the Whole

One more misuse of whole and part needs naming, because it operates at the scale of societies.

Interdependence is real. I depend on family, community, country, and a world of people I will never meet. None of these is Reality. As Chapter 9 argued, Reality is one by inclusion: nothing lies outside it. Every institution, nation, and group lies within it, as a part.

When a group claims to be the whole, it repeats at social scale the failure this book has traced since the prologue: a part standing for the whole. The group becomes the final judge of what is true. Loyalty to it outranks every other faithfulness. Dissent is treated as betrayal and correction as attack. Its members are valued for the functions they serve, which is the mirror failure, a whole reduced to a part.

Belonging to a group can be good. Chapter 4 called the healthy condition integration: connected and intact, participation without erasure. A group that can hear correction from its own members, and from outside, is behaving as a participant within Reality. A group that cannot has begun to claim a place no part can hold.

The same standard applies to this book. I have asked readers to stay open to justified correction, to judge behaviour rather than labels, and to refuse exemptions for themselves. If I protected this framework from correction, I would be repeating the pattern this chapter describes. The final chapter asks what would count as correcting it.

## Chapter 12: An Account Answerable to Reality

The interlude set the framework a continuing task: make its commitments clear enough that something could count as a reason to revise them. Chapter 11 asked what correction requires of a person. This chapter asks it of the book.

It is easy to build an account that cannot lose. Agreement supports it; disagreement shows that the critic misunderstood. Success confirms it; failure shows that the world resisted. I have put the point this way before: an ontology that cannot be wrong anywhere should not be trusted anywhere. **[AUTHOR: confirm reuse of this line from "A Test for Ontology" in your What Is Wholeness? draft]**

The remedy is not to hedge every claim until it says nothing. It is to say what kind of claim each one is, and what would support it, constrain it by narrowing where it could be true, or challenge it. Where a claim cannot yet be tested, I will say what would make it testable.

### Five Kinds of Claim

The book has used five kinds of statement, and they answer to different standards.

A *definition* says how a word will be used: here, *soul* names the singular point of view, the one whose experience this is. A definition is clear or unclear, useful or idle, rather than true or false. Its danger is that it can seem to settle what it only names. Calling the point of view a soul does not show that it is indivisible.

An *observation* reports what was found or experienced. The prologue is one: a surge begins in my chest, and widening my attention to my whole body helps. Testimony like this is good evidence about what happened to me, and weak evidence about why, or about anyone else.

A *philosophical inference* draws a conclusion from premises. The deck of cards in Chapter 2 is one: “one” answers how many decks, “fifty-two” answers how many cards, so being one and being many are compatible. It establishes that compatibility and no more: it does not show that any particular thing is a whole in the strict sense.

A *useful metaphor* is judged by what it helps us notice and by where it breaks. The black hole of the interlude fits attention gathering toward a center. It breaks at openness: a black hole is known for what it does not let back out, while the center this book describes can stay open to correction. The riverbed of Chapter 6 fits how repeated flow shapes a channel, not how a brain changes.

A *testable proposal* says something that observation could count against, and the book has few. Chapter 4 suggested that stronger inner integration lets a person sustain closer connection without losing themselves. Research on what psychologists call differentiation of self measures something close to this and could test it **[SOURCE: verify; Skowron and Friedlander 1998, the Differentiation of Self Inventory]**. Chapter 4's claim that health is a corner, not a midpoint, would fail if connection and autonomy, carefully measured, behaved as two ends of one trade-off.

The prologue's practice could be studied too: whether it helps others when one feeling floods their attention, and for whom it makes things worse, since body-focused attention can increase distress for some people **[SOURCE: verify; Treleaven, Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness, 2018]**. Such a study would test the practice, not the ontology: its success would not show that the soul is indivisible, nor its failure the opposite. The relief that opened this book motivates the inquiry. It does not settle it.

A sixth kind runs beneath the others: premises, including the values Chapter 11 rests on. People's experience, agency, and welfare matter. That is stated, not derived.

Most ways an account misleads its own author are confusions between these kinds: a premise treated as a result, a consequence of the framework's own premises treated as evidence from the world, a helpful image treated as a mechanism, a finding in a limited case treated as a law for every case. The last is the failure this book has named all along, a part standing for the whole, committed in an argument instead of a life. My working notes put it this way: **a part may suggest the whole; it may not announce the whole without testing the boundary it crossed.** **[AUTHOR: confirm wording; from your Pole Gap notes, July 2026]** One experience may suggest a practice, and several known layers of composition may suggest endless depth. Neither may announce its conclusion.

### The Center Under Test

Two proposals carry the most weight in this book: that a point of view is indivisible, and that consciousness is centered wholeness. Neither can be tested today.

The first is a philosophical inference, and Chapter 3 named its further premise: experiences had together are had by one subject, and that subject is not composed of parts. The premise would gain support if accounts without a center kept failing to say whose experience a given experience is. It would be challenged by an account without a center that explained unity, perspective, and ownership just as well, since the premise would then do no work. These are arguments, not measurements, until there is a way to recognize one center from outside.

The second cannot be tested as stated, because its criteria (a center, a boundary that gives an inside, an interior field, continuity) are not yet defined well enough to measure. An audit of my own working papers made exactly this criticism.

To make it testable, the criteria would have to be turned into measures and checked against cases where we have independent evidence: people awake, dreaming, in dreamless sleep, and under general anesthesia. Researchers already compare measures of how integrated the brain's activity is in such states with what people report afterwards **[SOURCE: verify; e.g., Casali et al., Science Translational Medicine, 2013]**. If measures built from this book's criteria tracked experience across those states, that would support the proposal. If they rated an anesthetized brain the same as a waking one, that would count against it.

Some limits would remain. If every centered whole has some inside, a cost Chapter 3 accepted openly, the inside of a cell lies beyond any check we could run. Each of us has exactly one case known from within. Where reports exist, the proposal can be made answerable. Beyond them, it can only be made consistent.

### Hard Cases

Chapter 2 offered a test for telling a single experiencer from a group, and Chapter 8 applied it: one indivisible center, versus a center-shaped region occupied by several. The test invites a probe: what happens when the thing is divided? A region occupied by several can be split and carried on in pieces; an indivisible center cannot. Each case below is a whole in the broad sense. The hard question is the strict one.

A city can be divided by a new border or merged with its neighbour, and its people go on. Its center, if it has one, is a set of purposes, institutions, and history negotiated among many, so by the test it looks like a region occupied by several.

A corporation can be split, and both halves can carry on with the same mission. The law treats it as a person for some purposes, and philosophers have argued that groups can be agents whose judgments differ from every member's **[SOURCE: verify; List and Pettit, Group Agency, 2011; a reference on corporate legal personality]**. But legal personhood is a definition made for a purpose, and agency, even if real, is not yet a point of view.

A relationship can end while two lives go on. My earlier drafts went both ways here: one said that a relationship becomes another whole, and a later one that there is nothing it is like to be a marriage. **[AUTHOR: confirm; both passages are in your drafts]** By the test, a relationship looks like a shared field with two centers. I hold that reading as the stronger one and the question as open. **[RULING R2: the test is offered as a test, not a verdict; your August 2026 groups record rules that a group is not a whole]**

A colony is harder. Some biologists call an ant or honeybee colony a superorganism, and a honeybee colony reproduces by dividing: part of it leaves with the old queen to found a new one **[SOURCE: verify; Hölldobler and Wilson, The Superorganism, 2009; Seeley, Honeybee Democracy, 2010]**. That suggests a region occupied by many. But a cell divides too, and by the cost Chapter 3 accepted, a cell may be a centered whole. So division alone cannot decide; the test would have to say whether, when a whole divides, one center becomes two or one ends and two begin.

A split-brain patient is the hardest case. After surgery that cuts the main connection between the brain's two halves, usually to control severe epilepsy, laboratory tests can show each half responding to information the other lacks, while in daily life the person usually presents as one. Whether there is one point of view or two is debated, and Thomas Nagel argued that the question may have no whole-number answer **[SOURCE: verify; Gazzaniga, Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2005; Nagel, "Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness," Synthese, 1971]**. The proposal of an indivisible center commits me to the opposite: points of view come only in whole numbers, even when no one can yet say how many are present. Here that commitment is most exposed.

The test works best where the answer was never much in doubt and worst where it matters most. What is missing is a way to recognize one center from outside that does not simply count bodies.

### A Shared Field or a Shared Experiencer

Chapter 8 proposed that minds take part in one field with distinct local organizations: we share a world and a field, not a point of view. The rival proposal says that a close relationship or a deeply coordinated group can become an experiencer of its own. What evidence could tell them apart?

Reports cannot settle it alone. Every report about what a group experiences comes from a member or from a procedure such as a vote. A group has no voice that does not pass through its members. That does not refute the rival proposal; it is why the question is hard.

Three kinds of evidence could still bear on it. The first is a criterion that works where we already know the answer: it would have to count one person as one, and two strangers in conversation as two. If it then found, in a deeply attuned group, a point of view beyond the members' own, that would favour a shared experiencer; if it found only the members', a shared field. The test above is meant to become such a criterion, and it is not one yet. The second is division, which the hard cases show can point the way without deciding.

The third is direct sharing. The proposal holds that connection between people stays mediated, through bodies, words, and a shared world. If two people could reliably have each other's private experience with no such channel, under conditions that rule out ordinary explanations, the proposal would fail. Nor could I rescue it by calling the field itself the channel. A field that explains whatever happens explains nothing, and the proposal of one field has to do more than rename what exists.

### Composition Without End

Chapter 9 weighed the reasons for and against the proposal that physical composition continues without a final layer. Here the question is what would count. **[RULING R10: presented as a hypothesis with reasons for and against, never as established]**

Support would come from structure found where none was expected. Atoms turned out to have parts, then their nuclei, then the protons and neutrons inside them **[SOURCE: a standard history of atomic and particle physics]**. Each finding fits the hypothesis. None proves it, any more than counting reaches a last number.

Constraint comes from searches that find nothing. Experiments have looked for parts inside electrons and quarks and found none down to the smallest distances they can probe **[SOURCE: verify; Particle Data Group, Review of Particle Physics, compositeness searches]**. That does not refute the hypothesis. It places any further structure below those limits.

A challenge would need more than an empty search: a well-supported theory saying why there is nothing below some level, for example one in which distances shorter than a certain length have no physical meaning. Physicists discuss whether the Planck length marks such a limit, and the question is open **[SOURCE: verify; e.g., Hossenfelder, Living Reviews in Relativity, 2013]**. If it were settled in favour of a true minimum with nothing organized beneath it, the downward half of the hypothesis would be refuted. Redefining composition after the fact, so that it no longer needed size or distance, would not save it; a hypothesis reworded to survive every finding has stopped saying anything. If the hypothesis is to reach below such a limit, as the interlude proposed, it needs a sense of structure that does not depend on distance, stated in advance together with what would count against it. Chapter 9 admitted that I do not yet have one.

The other half, composition everywhere, including in what looks empty, does not yet say what kind of structure fills apparent emptiness or how it would show itself. Until it does, it is a metaphysical hypothesis that guides where I look, not a finding. Either way, the proposed indivisible center is untouched, because it is not an extended thing.

### Correcting the Framework

Chapter 11 described protecting an image at the expense of truth. The account that cannot lose is that pattern in a framework, and two further signs deserve naming: terms that stretch to absorb each counterexample, and a vocabulary for failure turned on the people who object. If I answer an objection by describing the objector's state of mind instead of the objection's content, I have stopped examining the framework and started protecting it.

The ethics rests on stated values, which no measurement refutes, but its tools can be checked. If people using the book's distinctions kept finding defensiveness where careful observers found honest disagreement, the distinctions would be raising false alarms.

One correction in the framework's record concerns body and mind directly. Earlier versions described a whole as forming from a center, then a continuing thread, then the field of mind, and only then the boundary of the body. In June 2026 I reversed the last two, because there is no inside until something has closed around one: a boundary has to form before there is an interior for a mind to fill. The old order was marked as withdrawn rather than erased.

Even the correction needed correcting: it first went in without notice, against the framework's own rule against silent changes, and a review the same day caught the lapse. The mistaken order had also been tracking something real. Walk outward from the center of a ball and you pass its interior before you reach its surface: how a whole forms and how it is met from its center are two orders, and Chapter 3 keeps both. **[AUTHOR: confirm this telling]**

The framework's technical side, which this book does not present, has less comfortable entries. Most of its numerical matches with physics were found after the measurements were known. Those are fits, not predictions, because a method with enough freedom can match a number it already knows. A few claims were stated in advance in ways that can fail cleanly, and none had been settled when I wrote this. One holds that the Higgs boson is the only fundamental particle of its kind, so that discovering a second would falsify the framework's account of the particles. **[AUTHOR: confirm the record's status before publication; include or omit the particle-physics example]**

These are the conditions under which I would revise the book's central claims:

- If an account without an indivisible center explains everything the center was proposed to explain, I will give up indivisibility as a premise.
- If measures built from the book's criteria for an experiencing whole fail to track experience where it can be checked, I will revise the account of consciousness.
- If the proposal of one field predicts nothing that an account of separate minds in a shared world does not, I will call *field* a way of speaking. If people are shown to share experience with no mediating channel at all, I will drop the claim that connection stays mediated.
- If physics establishes a smallest meaningful length with nothing organized below it, I will treat endless downward composition as refuted.

The standing offer is simpler: if you show me a real error, I will retract in public and credit the correction. **[AUTHOR: drafted from the book's claims and your published corrigibility statement; confirm, revise, or replace these conditions]**

### Returning to the Opening Statement

Here is what each line of the opening statement now commits me to.

*I am whole* commits me to a life that is one by integration, many parts answering to one center, and one by continuity, moments that stay connected as the life changes. It claims neither perfection nor control.

*One whole body* commits me to a body that is one by distinction, organized in its own right, with real limits: the interface where I meet the world and the world meets me.

*One whole mind* commits me to a mind that is one by coherence, many relations in one interior, arising within the inside that the body's closure gives a life.

*One whole soul* commits me to the book's most exposed claim: a point of view that is one by indivisibility, so that points of view come only in whole numbers. It is a philosophical proposal, and I have said what would count against it.

*I am part* commits me to participation without erasure. Being part never reduces me to a part, and it gives no part the right to stand for the whole.

*One part of Earth* commits me to a dependence I did not choose and to limits I cannot wish away. Dependence alone creates no duty; my duties come from it together with values I have stated openly.

*One part of a greater whole* is the line this inquiry has made most careful. It names my participation in wider wholes, in the broad sense, and does not claim that any group I belong to has a point of view of its own. That question stays open.

*One part of Reality* commits me to a Reality that is one by inclusion: not a centered whole, not the greatest of wholes, not part of anything, and without a point of view of its own. Every thing within it is whole and part; Reality is what includes them. **[RULING R1: Reality is one by inclusion, not a centered whole; the title holds of every thing within Reality, and "part" keeps its meaning]**

In the prologue I wrote that the words are simple and their consequences require care. The words have not changed. I can say them now with their commitments in view and, where the account allows it, knowing what would show me wrong.

**Composition across scales.** The interlude introduces “infinitely finite” as a universal hypothesis: extended physical reality has further composition everywhere and at every depth. Explain how the proposal relates to an indivisible center of experience and to Reality as all-inclusive. Any claims about particles below the Planck scale, physical mind-fields, or the contents of a vacuum need an explicit physical account and evidence.

**Dimensions and recursion.** Preserve the philosophical meanings of center, path, boundary, field, and integrated whole. A plane circle has a one-dimensional circumference and a two-dimensional interior; a spherical boundary has a two-dimensional surface around a three-dimensional volume. Integration alone does not add a geometric coordinate. Representing a whole as one point at another scale leaves its internal organization intact.

**Ethics.** Being part of Reality does not, by itself, establish a duty. Show how claims about interdependence combine with stated values to support particular responsibilities. Do not identify the all-inclusive Reality with one institution, nation, or group.

**Usefulness and truth.** The author's experience of relief motivates the investigation. It does not establish the framework's metaphysical claims. A useful analogy can illuminate a relationship without proving that its proposed mechanism exists.

**Corrigibility and the defended image.** Explain how openness to reasons differs from automatic agreement, and how accountability can coexist with boundaries. The ethical comparison is with protecting an image at the expense of truth; a clinical account of narcissistic personality disorder would require separate research. Clarify what could count as correction of the framework itself, including evidence or arguments that would require revising its central claims.
